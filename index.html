Abstract

In March 2026 the U.S. authorized a 172-million-barrel release from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, structured as an exchange (loan-with-premium) rather than a sale, as its share of a coordinated 400-million-barrel IEA action against the Strait of Hormuz disruption. This paper reconstructs, from the EIA weekly inventory series, market spreads, and intraday tape, the gap between the program's announced parameters (172 mb; ~1.43 mb/day) and its realized execution (an SPR stock decline of ~84 mb through 19 Jun, ~0.86 mb/day over that stretch and ~0.79 mb/day for the programme to date, peaking near ~1.31 mb/day in the three record weeks of May, then easing to ~0.61 mb/day over the three most recent weeks). The release is shown to be physically incapable of clearing the net Hormuz shortfall on its own — on the paper's 9–13 mb/day house range, and equally on every published alternative (EIA implies ~16.7 mb/day of lost transit, Aramco ~14.3, the IEA ~4.3 of global supply). Its observable function, refined in v2, is active suppression: simultaneously pressing the prompt physical market (SPR barrels into spot) and the forward curve (announcement effect), aided by suppressed Chinese imports — three downward pressures on a market that would otherwise reveal the deficit. The compression is dated: remaining contracted SPR deliveries exhaust in late July / first week August at recent draw rates, which is also when independent analysts forecast Chinese demand to resume. The diplomatic resolution that would prevent reconciliation through dislocation has not arrived; a 60-day framework (ceasefire extension, Hormuz reopening, mine-clearing) has been tabled, sent back in early June over the maritime-control point, and then collapsed into renewed U.S.–Iran strikes by 9–11 June, with Iran re-declaring the strait closed (disputed by U.S. Central Command) while on the rebuilt Bloomberg basis the June curve did not flip: the contango compressed hard across grades (Brent +$4.48 on 5 Jun to +$1.29 on 19 Jun, dipping to −$0.55 in the week to 26 Jun; Dubai still +$6–7 throughout) while front-month Brent eased to ~$90 at settlement — so the June signal was compression rather than a prompt premium, and the genuine prompt premium arrived a fortnight later at the closed strait rather than across the whole complex. Then, on 11 June, the pendulum swung back: the President cancelled planned strikes and announced a "great settlement," a memorandum he himself called "a little conceptual." It resolved quickly: an interim MOU was declared complete ~14–15 June and signed electronically ~17 June at the G7 sidelines (the 19 June Switzerland technical round was then postponed, with a "roadmap" toward a final deal reached 21–22 June) — reopening Hormuz, lifting the blockades, opening a path to sanctions relief and holding out a potential ~$300bn reconstruction fund — contingent on a final nuclear agreement, envisaged as private-sector money from Gulf and east-Asian investors rather than a US grant (Axios, 16 Jun; PolitiFact, 22 Jun) — while deferring the nuclear file to a 60-day window. Brent collapsed ~14% on the week to ~$79 (−38% from the April peak), the whole curve repricing toward ~$80. The reopening is gradual — hundreds of ships stranded, mines to clear, 30 days to prewar traffic — so this is the optical branch the paper flagged, not a clean soft resolution; and the SPR draw held near its peak into the de-escalation — the first post-deal print (week ending 19 June, released 24 June) came in at −9.1 mb to 331.2 mb, the SPR's lowest since June 1983, even with the deal signed; the draw then tapered (−5.5, −6.2, −3.0 mb) to 316.5 mb by 10 July — the lowest since April 1983. But in that same fortnight the interim deal collapsed: the US revoked Iran's oil authorisation, struck Iran (8 and 12–14 July) and reinstated its naval blockade, Iran re-closed Hormuz, and front-month Brent snapped back to ~$83 (intraday high ~$86, ~+19% above pre-war) as transits fell >50%. The war has since widened — nine consecutive nights of US strikes, US troops killed in Iran's attack on a base in Jordan, and a Houthi maritime embargo on Saudi Arabia opening a new front — and Brent spiked to a $100.7 front-month peak (23 Jul, the highest since 2022) before a late-July strikes pause pulled it back to ~$87 as the SPR drained past 307.6 mb. In the first week of August the level drained out: Trump called off a planned mass strike (1 Aug), announced talks (3 Aug), and a 60-day Iran–Oman lane arrangement took shape — the front-month fell ~8% to ~$78 before Iran's own restrictive draft transit plan (6 Aug) steadied it at $80.46, and the Brent basis crossed into backwardation at −$2.03, its first sustained prompt premium of the year, joining a Dubai basis that had been negative since the 1 July roll. The second week took the level back and left the structure where it was: Iran announced a Hormuz route agreed with Oman that in its own telling bars US and Israeli vessels, fines violators 20% of cargo value and does not reopen the strait absent an end to the war, the blockade and the asset freeze; transits fell to eight ships on 11 Aug; and the front-month ran to $86.82 (12 Aug), +11.5% over six sessions, with the basis still at −$2.16 and Dubai at −$3.75. The SPR meanwhile broke 300 mb, drawing −6.1 mb to 298.7 mb (7 Aug) — the lowest since January 1983, with ~17 mb of contracted barrels left, and with DOE's own ledger showing no exchange-return barrels since February. With the buffer near its floor, the suppression can no longer hold the price down in either direction: what now moves crude is the diplomacy, and the paper's core claim — that the signal and the supply are spent in the same act — stands. The reconciliation the paper mapped is arriving as the paper said it would: in curve structure first, at the closed strait first, and as the barrels run out rather than after. The IEA put the same point in balance terms on 12 August — global observed inventories down 410 mb since the war, a Q3 deficit of 1.8 mb/day, and the warning that “the urgency of reopening the Strait has increased, as previously available inventory buffers are rapidly depleting.” The crude reconciliation, the SPR contracted exhaustion, and the contested ceasefire window still converge on the same six-to-eight-week period — a configuration the paper terms the contested deadline — but the dislocation has begun arriving early, via diplomatic breakdown rather than buffer exhaustion.

01Summary

The argument in one screen — v2.11

A strategic reserve is a stock; a supply crisis is a problem of flow. The 2026 SPR release cannot resolve the deficit at any plausible delivery rate; what it can do is buy time by pressing both ends of the futures curve down at once. v2 evidences this directly.

(i) The headline is a ceiling, not a delivery — and the market stopped taking the barrels. Of 172 mb authorized, ~133.5 mb contracted across five tenders, and an SPR stock decline of ~84 mb through 19 Jun. (That decline is the paper's proxy for deliveries and is not identical to audited liftings — title transfer and physical lifting need not fall in the same week — so the “delivered” framing carried in v1–v2.10 is retired in favour of “stock decline.”) New in v2.11: the fifth tender failed. DOE issued an RFP on 10 Jun 2026 for a further 40 mb (Bryan Mound, Big Hill), bids due 15 Jun; it awarded 0.5 mb — 1.25% of the volume offered — to Vitol, leaving 39.5 mb unawarded, after near-term crude fell from ~$90 to ~$80 between launch and bid date and the calendar spread that makes an exchange worth bidding for collapsed with it. No tender has been issued since. That single fact reframes the taper: the programme has been effectively stalled since mid-June not because DOE chose to wind it down but because the exchange stopped clearing. Realized lift averaged ~0.79 mb/day across the whole programme (116.75 mb of stock decline over the 147 days from the 13 Mar announcement to the 7 Aug print) and ~0.86 mb/day through 19 Jun; the three record weeks of May reached ~1.31 mb/day against the advertised 1.43, and the three most recent weeks average only ~0.61. All four rates are recomputed from DOESSPR in v2.11; v2.10 carried ~0.9 and 0.72 from different, unstated windows.

(ii) The drawdown ran at peak rate, briefly eased, then re-accelerated. Weekly draws of −8.6, −9.9, −9.1, −8.0, −7.9, then −8.9 mb (12 Jun), −9.1 mb (19 Jun), −5.5 mb (26 Jun), −6.2 mb (3 Jul) −3.0 mb (10 Jul) −5.1 mb (17 Jul), −3.8 mb (24 Jul), −2.8 mb (31 Jul) and −6.1 mb (7 Aug) to 298.7 mb — the first sub-300 mb print since January 1983, ~117 mb drawn since the war began. The dip to ~8 mb (late May/early June) looked like a benign taper; the week to 12 Jun put that to rest, re-accelerating as the war premium collapsed and the deal came together. DOE drawing faster as the crisis resolves is the opposite of a supply remedy winding down — it reads as pressing the price down or rushing to finish deliveries. The draw continued through the late-July pause and into the August talks; with the buffer near its floor (~17 mb contracted left after the 7 Aug print) the suppression is no longer what sets the price — Brent round-tripped ~$94 → ~$82 → ~$87, fell to ~$79 (5 Aug) and $80.46 (6 Aug) on the diplomacy, then ran back to $86.82 (12 Aug), +7.9% on the week and +11.5% over six sessions, easing to $85.94 (13 Aug) — all of it on the negotiating table, none of it on DOE.

(iii) Deep contango for five months — then a prompt premium, at the strait first and in both basins now. On the Bloomberg futures basis (front-month minus spot; positive = contango, negative = backwardation), the grades nearest Hormuz showed the prompt crushed below the future from the war onset through June — Dubai front-month peaking +$38.40 above spot (20 Mar) and still $11–15 above in late May, outright contango — the mask of a deficit held down by SPR supply and absent Chinese demand. Through June the contango compressed rather than flipped (Brent +$4.48 → +$1.29 → −$0.55 into 26 Jun; Dubai +$6.72 → +$7.08 → +$11.68 into the expiring contract). Then the basins separated. Dubai flipped at the 1 July roll and has stayed negative: −$2.02 (2 Jul), −$3.60 (16 Jul), a −$9.35 trough at the war peak (23 Jul), −$3.03 (30 Jul), −$4.64 (6 Aug), −$3.75 (12 Aug). Brent held its contango right through the July escalation (+$6.43 on 23 Jul, +$2.15 on 30 Jul) — the Atlantic basin still cushioned by the barrels DOE could actually reach it with — and crossed only in August: −$2.03 (6 Aug), −$2.16 (12 Aug). The outright level (CO1) round-tripped from ~$71.80 (2 Jul) to a $100.69 peak (23 Jul), to $89.03 (30 Jul), $80.46 (6 Aug) and back to $86.82 (12 Aug) / $85.94 (13 Aug). So the level and the structure are now moving together, in the same direction, at the moment the reserve fell below 300 mb. Note the asymmetry the paper has flagged throughout, and note that it has finally resolved the way the thesis predicted: the strait is still shut, the reserve is nearly empty, and the curve has stopped pricing comfort. That is a physical fact arriving in a positioning instrument, which is the sequence the paper has argued for since v2.

(iv) The contested deadline resolved — by a deal, not by exhaustion. The 60-day MOU (19 Jun) collapsed into war, now in a fragile pause. After the 31 Jul print ~17 mb of contracted SPR remains (~117 mb lifted) — roughly three to five weeks at the ~3–6 mb/week pace of the last month — with the caveat, new in this revision, that DOE's ledger names a Release No. 3 that has not yet moved a barrel, so the contracted residual could be larger than the tender arithmetic implies — a near-empty backstop even as the talks resume: neither the pause nor the August diplomacy has refilled the reserve or reopened the strait. So the diplomatic clock stopped first. What remains live is the physical reopening clock — 30 days to prewar Hormuz traffic, mines to clear, ~600 ships to move — and the buffer clock as a backstop if that reopening stalls.

(v) Retail products are a separate, structural clock — now feeding the macro. US average gasoline (BBG) is ~$4.72 (11 Aug), down only marginally from ~$4.76 at end-July (still below the $5.47 all-time high on the same BBG series) — the crude round-trip has not reached the pump, atop the structural refining picture (seven closures since 2019 per consultancy tallies rather than an EIA count; finished-fuel inventories forecast to 2000-era lows — an EIA projection dated March 2025 off the February 2025 STEO, i.e. a pre-war baseline). One component of that picture has moved and the paper corrects it here: refinery utilization is not ~92% but ~96% — 96.1% in the week to 19 Jun on the EIA's own weekly report, and 96.2–96.5% in late July. Refiners are running harder than the ~92% figure carried since v1 implied, which makes the inventory picture tighter on demand and looser on spare capacity than the paper has been saying. Energy-led U.S. inflation peaked at 4.2% year-on-year in May — the highest since April 2023 — and has since eased: the July CPI, released 12 Aug, came in at 3.4% (from 3.5% in June), with core at 2.5%, both softer than expected. The energy-driven burst earlier in the year is unwinding at the headline level even as crude has rallied 12% in the past six sessions, which is the lag the paper has flagged throughout: the structural refining deficit means neither a crude resolution nor a crude rally reaches the pump on the timeline the macro data implies. Products remain the slow, separate clock.

The SPR exchange is not failing to be a supply remedy by accident — it was never structured to be one. Its function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market down with finite physical barrels and the forward curve down with announcement effects, in service of buying time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. When the barrels run out, the signal dies with them.

The contested deadline has now resolved — by a signed deal (19 Jun), ahead of the buffer. But the resolution is optical in shape: a 14-point MOU that reopens Hormuz on paper, opens a sanctions-relief path and dangles a conditional ~$300bn reconstruction fund (contingent on a final nuclear deal, and private rather than US money), and defers the nuclear file 60 days. Tehran formally suspended its MOU commitments on 18 July. The clocks did not all stop. The U.S. supply-buffer clock keeps ticking (SPR drawn to 298.7 mb — the lowest since January 1983 — ~17 mb of contracted barrels left, and the draw re-accelerated to −6.1 mb in the week to 7 Aug as the talks hardened) and the Iranian revenue/blockade clock (export losses every day Hormuz stays shut) — which the deal is meant to stop. The market that spent late May pricing the soft outcome got it on 14–19 June and ran with it: Brent −14% on the week, the whole curve to ~$80. The scoreboard turned toward resolution. The catch the paper has flagged from the start is that a signed, conceptual MOU is not the same as oil moving freely through Hormuz — and the deal has now collapsed with the buffer nearly spent: the US struck Iran again (8 & 12–14 Jul), reinstated its naval blockade and Iran re-closed the strait, so the suppression can no longer cap the price — Brent round-tripped to a $100.7 front-month peak, dipped to ~$82 on the pause, and sits ~$83 as a second, narrower arrangement is negotiated. The August draft is the same catch in sharper form: a lane scheme that reopens Hormuz on paper under Iranian and Omani management, with Iran's own text barring US and Israeli ships and fining violators 20% of cargo value, is not oil moving freely.

02Thought Process

How the question unfolded — extended in v2

Stage 1 — The accounting question

Was the exchanged oil still booked as SPR inventory while physically gone? Directionally the instinct was right (an exchange retains a government claim recorded as a receivable), but the suspicion landed in the wrong ledger. The EIA series is physical and draws down on lifting.

Stage 2 — The execution forensics

Reading the tenders against the weekly inventory series revealed the awarded-versus-delivered gap and the realized lift rate — roughly half the advertised pace for most of the program, only reaching the plan in its latest weeks. The advertised "1.43 mb/day" was real as a plan; delivery only approached it as the program matured.

Stage 3 — The scale test

Setting realized lift against the net Hormuz shortfall (~9–13 mb/day on this paper's house range; see the Figure 2 note for the published alternatives, which run from ~4.3 to ~16.7 mb/day depending on definition) showed the release covers a fraction of the gap for a limited duration. A physical remedy it is not, on any of those definitions.

Stage 4 — The mechanism & the clock

If not supply, then signal: the program suppresses the forward curve and buys time. That reframes "when does it end?" as "when are the physical and expectations markets forced to reconcile?" — a question answered by the prompt-versus-deferred spread.

Stage 5 (new in v2) — The suppression mechanism, visible

The late-May spread data and the SPR prints together force the v2 conclusion. The Gulf-grade curve did not behave as v1's "convergence" framing predicted — through late May the prompt was pressed below the future (Dubai front-month $11–16 over spot), outright contango, while DOE drew at the design ceiling: both spot and futures pressed down at once — SPR barrels into the prompt market, forward-curve guidance for the paper market, suppressed Chinese demand removing global pull. The contested deadline has now settled that question the other way. The early-June compression was not a managed hand-off to a real deal; within days the framework was sent back, the talks collapsed, and the war resumed. On the rebuilt series what the June tape shows is the contango narrowing rather than inverting — Brent +$4.48 (5 Jun) → +$1.29 (19 Jun) → −$0.55 (26 Jun), Dubai holding +$6–12 up to the roll — with outright Brent easing to ~$90 over the same days. v2.5 read that narrowing as a completed flip and later versions compounded it with an inverted sign; the rebuilt series shows the suppression was still holding the prompt down through June and then gave way basin by basin: Dubai at the 1 July roll, Brent only in August. That sequencing is a stronger version of the same argument, because it tracks physical reach — DOE could keep pressing the Atlantic prompt long after it had lost the Gulf, and DOE's own ledger shows it doing exactly that, 39.4 mb in May and 33.0 mb in June against 6.1 mb so far in August. The calm was bought; when the diplomacy the barrels were buying time for failed to arrive, the suppression began to give way to the tightness it had deferred — surfacing in the structure, via diplomatic breakdown ahead of, not because of, the buffer running dry. The resolution then arrived the way the paper had marked most likely and most under-priced: not a clean soft settlement but an interim, optical one — a signed MOU that reopens Hormuz on paper, pays Iran, and defers the nuclear file — on which the market nonetheless priced a full resolution, collapsing crude ~14% on the week to ~$79. And in the final irony for the supply-remedy story, DOE did not ease into the de-escalation; it drew faster (−8.9 mb) as the war premium came out — which the post-deal print (19 Jun) has now answered: it held at peak, −9.1 mb to 331.2 mb.

Stage 6 (new in v2) — The contested deadline

If the suppression is active and finite, then its duration is the strategic variable. Both negotiating parties can compute the buffer-exhaustion date from public data. Iran's incentive is to delay any concession until that date approaches and U.S. leverage decays; the U.S. incentive (per Trump's "we'll out-wait him") is to deny that any concession is forthcoming on Iran's clock. The leaked-but-mutually-disowned MOU was sized to 60 days — almost exactly the remaining contracted SPR delivery window. The market structure and the diplomatic structure are now visibly synchronized to the same date.

The prompt-physical spread — from suppression to a prompt premium

The deep Gulf-grade contango is the cleanest single piece of evidence in the paper. A genuine multi-million-barrel-a-day net Hormuz deficit — 9–13 mb/day on this paper's range, ~14 on Aramco's — cannot coexist with the grades nearest Hormuz trading $11–16 below their own front-month. The only way both are true at once is if the prompt is being masked by active physical supply (SPR barrels reaching the spot window) and absent demand (China) — both dated, both finite. On the Bloomberg basis the effect is larger than the prior Dated read showed. The early-June unwind toward backwardation, occurring as the whole complex falls rather than rises, is consistent with the suppression being relaxed into a market pricing de-escalation — not with a deficit reasserting. That distinction is the soft-vs-hard fork, and it turns on whether a deal actually signs.

The war-premium round-trip

The clearest picture of the whole episode is the outright front-month itself (Bloomberg CO1). Brent began the year in the low-$60s on an oversupplied, pre-war market; the 28 February war and the Hormuz closure drove it to a $112.6 front-month peak (27 Mar) (Dated Brent spiked far higher); the SPR release, suppressed Chinese demand and recurring deal-hope then ground it back down through the spring; the 14–19 June agreement collapsed the remaining premium, returning the front-month to ~$72 by early July; but the deal broke down — US strikes, the naval blockade reinstated, Hormuz re-closed — and the premium snapped back and surged to a $100.7 front-month peak (23 July) as the war widened, before a late-July strikes pause knocked it to $89.0 (30 July). The first week of August took it to $80.5 (6 Aug) on the called-off strike and the announced talks. The second week took it back: with the Oman route agreed but conditioned, transits down to eight on 11 Aug and both sides hardening, it ran to $86.8 (12 Aug), +11.5% over six sessions, easing to $85.9 on 13 Aug. Wire copy carried that 23 July peak as ~$94 and the 12 August close as $86.82; both track the COA column. The IEA brackets the range from the other side: Dated Brent printed $105 on 23 July and ended the month at $96.80. Dated above front-month above the wire quote is the ordering a genuinely tight prompt market produces, and it is the ordering the rebuilt series shows.

On 28 May, WTI front-month and Brent front-month rallied in lockstep on truce headlines to +4%, then faded together as the framework was disowned. The two benchmarks moved as one across the session — the signature of a market pricing a single global signal (the diplomatic clock) rather than independent fundamentals. The fade pattern repeats the cycle observed since March: rally on resolution-hope, fade as substance fails to arrive. Each cycle consumes barrels and credibility.

05Outlook — Two clocks, one window

The contested deadline has passed — what binds now is the reopening regime

The v1 framing — soft landing vs. hard reconciliation — is preserved but reorganized. The decisive variable is no longer "when do buffers exhaust" in the abstract; it is whose clock binds first in the contested deadline.

The U.S. supply-buffer clock

Remaining contracted SPR deliveries: ~17 mb of the ~133.5 mb awarded after the −6.1 mb week to 7 Aug. At the ~3–6 mb/week pace of the last month that would exhaust in ~3–5 weeks — and there is no sign of replacement, since the June tender for a further 40 mb drew bids on only 0.5 mb and none has been issued since. The official forecast moved with the physical picture: the EIA's August Short-Term Energy Outlook (11 Aug) raised its 2026 Brent forecast to $87 from $82 and its 3Q26 forecast to $85, explicitly because severe Hormuz shipping restrictions are now assumed to persist at least through August — but the buffer stopped being the binding clock some time ago; it is now a backstop, and a thin one. Behind it, the operational floor — revised to ~200 mb on the Tecity in-house estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb level conventionally cited — sits only ~99 mb below the current 298.7 mb stock (the lowest since January 1983); commercial crude has been drawing alongside it: total US crude stocks (commercial plus SPR) hit 730.8 mb on 3 Jul, reported as the lowest since 1984, and have fallen further since — 407.0 mb commercial plus 298.7 mb SPR is 705.7 mb as of 7 Aug. There is materially less cushion behind the SPR than the standard floor implies, and ~25 mb less than the figure v2.10 carried. Independent forecast (DeLaura, Rabobank): Chinese demand resumes mid-July, removing the third suppressor. That forecast can now be marked, and it was broadly right on timing and only half right on effect: China's July crude imports rebounded ~22% month-on-month to about 8.45 mb/day, a three-month high, after June printed a near-decade low — but they remain roughly a quarter below July 2025, and the rebound leaned on non-Middle-East barrels (notably Russian ESPO) rather than on Gulf supply. The third suppressor is lifting, not lifted. What v2.3 changes is the trigger: the dislocation no longer waits on buffer exhaustion. The 9–11 June swings — collapse and re-closure, then a sudden deal announcement — show the reconciliation can be forced (or deferred) by headlines well before the buffer empties, with stock still ~25 mb from contracted-empty. The buffer clock now sets the ceiling on how long any renewed suppression could even be attempted, not the timing of the break, which diplomacy is driving day to day.

The first two weeks of August are the cleanest demonstration yet of that last point. With ~17 mb of contracted barrels left — too little to move a global market — crude fell ~8% in three sessions on a called-off strike and an announced negotiation, rose 3.8% in one session on a parliamentary draft, and then rose 11.5% over six sessions (to $86.82 on 12 Aug) when the Oman route arrived conditioned rather than free and transits fell to eight on 11 Aug. Not one of those moves had anything to do with DOE — which drew its largest weekly volume in five weeks over the same fortnight. The buffer no longer sets the price; it only sets how badly the United States would be exposed if the negotiation fails, and that exposure is now roughly three to five weeks of contracted deliveries and ~99 mb of cushion above the Tecity floor.

The global buffer, not just the American one

The paper has tracked one inventory series because it is the one the United States controls. The IEA's August Oil Market Report (12 Aug) puts it in the global frame, and the numbers are worse than the SPR's alone. Observed global oil inventories fell 69 mb in July to below 7.9 bn barrels, the lowest since April 2025, and are down a cumulative 410 mb since the war began — an average draw of ~2.7 mb/day. The US SPR's 116.7 mb is roughly 28% of that: a single national reserve accounting for more than a quarter of the world's destocking, which is a compact restatement of this paper's thesis in someone else's data. The IEA now sees a 1.8 mb/day deficit in the third quarter, more than double the ~0.8 mb/day it projected a month earlier, and cut 2026 supply by 4.3 mb/day to ~102 mb/day while cutting demand by 1.6 mb/day — the balance is being closed by demand destruction, not by barrels. Gulf production has recovered to 23.9 mb/day but remains 8.3 mb/day below pre-war, and regional exports including bypass routes fell 2.1 mb/day in July to 15 mb/day. The agency's own conclusion is the one this paper has been building toward: “The urgency of reopening the Strait has increased, as previously available inventory buffers are rapidly depleting.” Two cautions on splicing these into the paper's own arithmetic. The IEA's 1.8 mb/day is a residual balance after demand destruction and rerouting; the paper's 9–13 mb/day is gross transit at risk. They are not competing estimates of the same quantity, and the gap between them is exactly the demand destruction and bypass capacity that a year of the war has forced into existence.

Which floor? Three numbers now in circulation

The floor question stopped being academic this fortnight, and the public numbers do not agree. (a) The statutory reference is 252.4 mb — the level constraining the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority under the Energy Policy and Conservation Act. It does not bind presidential emergency drawdowns or the exchange authority under which this programme runs, so it is a political marker rather than an operating limit; at 298.7 mb the reserve is ~46 mb above it. (b) DOE told reporters in July that the true cavern-mechanics floor is around 10% of capacity, ~70 mb — a redefinition that, taken at face value, roughly triples the notional cushion relative to the 250–300 mb range the industry has long treated as the practical limit. (c) This paper continues to use the ~200 mb Tecity in-house operational estimate (analyst Zilin), on the view that a reserve capable only of trickling barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer regardless of what remains in them. The GAO finding supports that scepticism directly: drawdown capability was already at 61% of design (2.70 against 4.415 mb/day) in December 2025, before ~117 mb came out, and the capacity to accept returning crude was at 56% of specification — which matters more than it used to, because DOE's own ledger (Figure 5) shows the entire repayment leg of this exchange is still outstanding. A floor defined by what is in the salt is not the same as a floor defined by what can reach a refinery in a fortnight. Readers who prefer DOE's number should read the paper's cushion as ~229 mb rather than ~99 mb, and should then ask the GAO question about how fast any of it could move.

The Iranian revenue/blockade clock

New in v2.11: this clock has effectively stopped ticking, because the exports have stopped. Loading operations at Kharg Island — roughly 90% of Iran's crude exports — halted on 31 July and had not resumed as of 11 Aug. Satellite imagery shows all three berths empty for an extended period (Windward), with a growing queue of laden tankers loitering along the Iranian coast; Kpler and Energy Aspects both date the stoppage to 31 Jul, and the FT reported no loadings for a week under the blockade. An export clock that has run to zero is no longer a decaying source of leverage over time — it is a fixed daily loss, which is precisely the condition under which Tehran's incentive shifts from waiting to extracting terms, and is consistent with the hardened four-part condition list of 11 Aug. Iran is bleeding export revenue every day Hormuz stays shut and the U.S. naval blockade holds. In late May — three weeks before the MOU was signed, and as a precondition for it — Iran demanded immediate release of $12bn of its funds held in Qatar, half of a $24bn total with the balance inside 60 days: liquidity to span its clock. (v2.10 described this as a post-MOU request, which was wrong on both date and framing.) The demand has since hardened into Rezaei's 11 Aug condition list. Aramco's ~100 mb/week global supply loss is matched by Iran's own stranded exports. Iran's clock binds whenever its FX/revenue position becomes politically unsustainable, which is unobservable from outside but is on its own decay path.

The three endgames

Soft (diplomatic, full) — partially realized, execution pending: the 60-day MOU signs and delivers genuine free passage (no tolls, mines cleared, insurers treating the strait as open), crude reconciles to the deferred curve's ~$65–70 anchor, products stay structurally elevated. This was the late-May base case the ~20% crude drop priced; it receded into the 9–10 June strikes, then returned on 11 June when the President announced a "great settlement" and oil fell ~3%. But the full soft case requires the signed text to deliver genuine free passage — and the announced MOU is "conceptual," unconfirmed by Iran, with Israel not a party. So the tape is again pricing a deal, but whether it is the soft deal (real reopening) or merely the optical one is exactly the unresolved fork.

Middle (optical) — this is what landed: the MOU signs and headline-reopens Hormuz, but the reopening is partial — escorted, toll-laden, slow-to-clear-mines, or kept under Iranian "management" — so physical free flow lags the announcement. Futures rally on the signing; the suppression props (SPR, China) lift on schedule into a market whose physical deficit hasn't actually closed. The market briefly believes the deal is the resolution before discovering it is not. This is the configuration the curve is least equipped to recognize, and v2.2 raises its weight: the live sticking point is precisely maritime control, and an experienced commodities voice (ICMA's Parker) has publicly warned that even if Hormuz opens, the opening will likely be only partial, citing refinery damage, depleted inventories, and tanker-security frictions. The reported terms (no tolls, 30-day mine-clearing) describe the soft case; whether the signed text and the physical execution deliver it is the fork. This is what landed. The signed MOU (19 Jun) reopens Hormuz on paper and the market priced a clean resolution — Brent to ~$79, the curve to ~$80 — but the physical substance (escort terms, mine-clearing, insurer confidence, ~600 stranded ships, a 30-day timeline) is only beginning. The paper had flagged this as the under-priced case; the gap between a signed conceptual MOU and oil actually moving freely is exactly where the residual risk now sits. The August negotiation has made that gap explicit rather than closing it. The reported 60-day Iran–Oman arrangement is a managed-lane regime by construction — inbound through Iranian waters, outbound through Omani waters, a joint coordination centre, the mined median lane deferred 30 days — and Iran's own published draft would bar US and Israeli ships and fine violators 20% of cargo value. That is the optical branch written into the text of the deal itself, not merely a risk that the execution disappoints. An outside forecast (Stavridis, 4 Aug) independently put this “dual-key” structure at the modal outcome of the ~60% negotiation path. If it signs, the correct reading of a futures rally is not that the deficit closed but that the market has agreed to price a permitted, partial, politically revocable flow as though it were free passage.

Hard (physical) — flared 9–10 Jun, now dormant; the live tail-risk is a reopening that stalls: the 9–10 June collapse pushed hard toward this branch before the buffer ran dry — though on the rebuilt series the June curve had only compressed, not detached (Brent +$2.67, Dubai +$6.39 on 11 Jun) — and the deal then collapsed the level instead. The branch is no longer dormant. It has arrived in the form the paper specified: a prompt premium in the grade at the strait since the 1 July roll (Dubai −$9.35 at the trough, −$3.75 on 12 Aug), Brent joining it in August (−$2.03 → −$2.16), and the outright following rather than leading (+11.5% over six sessions to $86.82). One analyst is on the record warning of a move into the mid-to-high $100s later this summer if normal transit does not materialise; the July print of $100.7 on CO1 and $105 on Dated is the working evidence that the tape can get there on headlines alone. The distinctive v2.3 point stands and has strengthened: the branch was triggered by diplomatic breakdown rather than SPR exhaustion — but the two clocks have now converged, with ~17 mb of contracted barrels left, so the next dislocation lands into a market with neither a cushion nor a hedge. That is the source of the violence if it continues.

The product clock — running independently underneath

Whichever crude branch resolves, U.S. refined products are on their own structural clock: EIA-forecast 2026 inventories at 2000-era lows (a March 2025 projection off the February 2025 STEO — a pre-war baseline, not crisis-informed guidance), utilization running at ~96% rather than the ~92% carried in earlier versions, effectively no spare capacity, and seven major closures since 2019 on consultancy tallies. A clean Hormuz deal cannot deliver consumer relief on the timeline a soft-landing crude resolution would imply. This decouples the political payoff of a deal from the diplomatic incentive to sign one — and arguably reduces the threshold at which the U.S. accepts unfavorable terms, since the deal does not buy as much retail relief as in past precedent.

06Risk Assessment

What would confirm or falsify the suppression and contested-deadline reads

The suppression thesis has now fully played out, and the rebuilt series makes the finish cleaner than the first cut of v2.11 reported it. The draw ran to a 43-year low and then through it — −6.1 mb to 298.7 mb (7 Aug), below 300 for the first time since January 1983, the largest weekly draw in five weeks, printed in the week the route talks peaked. The interim deal collapsed, the war widened, and the front-month spiked to $100.7 (23 Jul) with Dated at $105. With ~17 mb of contracted barrels left the reserve is effectively spent, and the fortnight to 13 August showed what that means: the price fell ~8% on a called-off strike, then rose 11.5% over six sessions as Iran priced a reopening at ending the war, lifting the blockade and releasing frozen assets — and, decisively, the curve moved with it. Dubai has priced a prompt premium since the 1 July roll; Brent joined it on 6 Aug and held at −$2.16 on 12 Aug. Level and structure are now pointing the same way for the first time since the war began. The paper's central prediction is realized in the order it specified: the barrels ran out, the mask came off at the closed strait first and the Atlantic second, and the tightness surfaced in structure as the buffer emptied rather than after. DOE's own ledger adds the confirmation the weekly series could not: 116.6 mb released between March and August against zero return barrels since February. Once the barrels ran low, the price detached from DOE and attached to the negotiating table. The open question is no longer whether the suppression ends but whether the market is now under-pricing a strait that remains physically shut while the curve prices comfort.

Tells the soft branch is recovering. A signed MOU (not a "fully negotiated" claim amid strikes) with the maritime-control point resolved and mines clearing on schedule; a sustained ceasefire holding through more than a single news cycle; the outright price falling back toward the high-$80s; gasoline resuming its decline once the escalation washes through.

Tells the hard branch is winning (most now present). Talks stalled and the strait re-declared closed until a list of preconditions is met (present); both basins now in backwardation — Dubai since the 1 Jul roll, Brent since 6 Aug (present, and this is the leading structural tell the paper named); the backwardation broadening across basins rather than sitting in one (present as of this fortnight); outright Brent turning up with the structure rather than against it (present — CO1 +11.5% over six sessions to $86.82 on 12 Aug); SPR draws snapping back toward the high single digits as the floor nears (partially present — the 7 Aug week re-accelerated to −6.1 mb, the largest in five weeks, though short of 9); global inventories drawing at ~2.7 mb/day with the IEA's Q3 deficit doubling to 1.8 mb/day (present, new this week); Cushing toward minimums and total US crude stocks at 730.8 mb, the lowest since 1984 (present); product cracks blowing out — Atlantic Basin refining margins at record highs on the IEA's August read, with diesel exports from Russia, the Middle East and Asia down 1.3 mb/day, about 20% of global seaborne diesel trade (present, new this week); energy-led inflation turning back up from the 3.4% July print (peak 4.2% in May) as the August crude rally passes through (not yet, and the pump is the laggard the paper has flagged throughout).

Optical-deal risk (the under-priced middle case). A signed MOU that headline-reopens Hormuz without delivering physical free passage would trigger a futures rally while leaving the deficit broadly intact. Detection signal: a deal is announced but the post-roll Dubai/Oman spread does not normalize and tanker transits stay thin — the Asian physical market shows the truth first, because partial/escorted/tolled reopening appears in transit counts before headlines. Watch the Hormuz transit tally and the Dubai/Oman spread as the two independent verifiers of any announcement. ICMA's Parker has already flagged a partial opening as the likely case.

Complacency risk — retired, and replaced. The first cut of v2.11 named complacency the primary risk on the basis of a Brent contango reading that this revision corrects; on the rebuilt series the market did not stop paying for prompt barrels, and by 6–12 August it was paying more for them in both basins while the outright rallied 11.5%. The falsification test v2.10 set has now fired on both legs: the lane arrangement did not deliver free passage, transits fell to eight ships on 11 Aug and a five-day average near a three-month low, the level returned, and the structure turned. What replaces complacency is its mirror: exhaustion risk. The prompt premium is surfacing into a market with no remaining buffer — ~17 mb of contracted SPR, a reserve below 300 mb with no return barrels booked since February, drawdown capability at 61% of design per GAO, global inventories down 410 mb since the war and an IEA Q3 deficit of 1.8 mb/day, eight transits a day through Hormuz, and a second chokepoint at Bab al-Mandeb now taking lethal fire. The test from here is specific and cheap to watch, and it is now the reverse of the one v2.10 set: if the lane arrangement signs and transits climb out of single digits within two to three weeks, the backwardation should compress and the soft branch returns. If Iran's preconditions harden into the operative position and transits stay at eight a day past the reserve's contracted exhaustion, the prompt premium should deepen with nothing left to damp it — and that is the configuration the paper has said all along would produce the violent move.

Positioning risk. A forward curve still anchored to resolution — the long end pinned near ~$65–70 — leaves the market structurally under-hedged for failure. The precursor the paper described is now in the tape rather than pending: the front has flipped to a prompt premium in both basins (Brent −$2.16, Dubai −$3.75) while the long end holds its anchor and the outright has recovered to $86.82. A curve that has turned at the front but not repriced the back is the classic pre-dislocation shape, and it is the shape the SPR is no longer able to flatten. In June the structure warned and the level lagged; in August they have moved together, which removes the ambiguity that let the market read June as noise.

Head-fake risk (now bilateral). The rally-fade-deny cycle repeated since March and reached its sharpest form on 11 Jun — but this time it terminated in an actual signing (19 Jun), so the head-fake risk has largely discharged. The residual is the mirror image: a signed but conceptual/interim MOU is now being treated as a done deal, when the physical reopening (mines, escorts, insurers, ~600 stranded ships) is what determines whether the priced resolution holds. Discount both "deal imminent" and definitive "strait closed/open" claims (Iran asserted closure; US CENTCOM denied) until confirmed by both sides and by tanker-transit data, not headlines.

Escalation risk. Combat continued through the talks (US strikes in southern Iran; Iranian ballistic missiles toward Kuwait), raising the probability that the U.S. abandons negotiation for force if the clock runs out — which removes Iran's leverage but does not necessarily reopen Hormuz quickly.

Partial-reopening risk. An intermittent, escorted, tolled, or "Iranian-managed" reopening smears the binary into a prolonged muddy tightening, stretching the timeline and blunting both the supply-buffer urgency and the diplomatic incentive to settle. This is the same configuration as the optical branch and is now the live sticking point.
