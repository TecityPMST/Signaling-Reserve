What changed — week to 17 September
  • The only large bypass was shut. Drones from Iraqi territory struck the Saudi East–West (Petroline) pipeline on 10–11 September, damaging at least two pumping stations; Riyadh closed it on the 11th. No Saudi crude has departed Yanbu since.
  • The Red Sea end closed too — then partly reversed. The Houthis took Mocha on the 10th and Dhubab, the Hanish islands and Perim on the 11th; by the 14th there was no sign of a continuing presence on Perim, with government forces counter-attacking. Bab el-Mandeb is contested, not held.
  • Brent broke $100, then found a way round. The front-month ran to $103.31 (15 Sep), easing to $99.56 after Aramco said it aims to restore half of Petroline within days and began shipping crude to Asia via ship-to-ship transfers off Sohar — out through the strait the pipeline existed to avoid. Both premiums still deepened: Brent −$5.07, Dubai −$15.28.
  • The draw has stopped; Washington is talking refill. The reserve fell just −1.6 mb in two weeks to 285.0 mb, lowest since November 1982, against −6.1 mb a single week in early August; ~3 mb of contracted barrels remain. On 14 September Energy Secretary Wright said refilling “will start in the next few months,” seeking some $20bn.
  • Supply cut again; the Fed turned. The IEA cut 2026 world supply by 5.7 mb/day on 11 September. On the 16th the Fed raised rates 25bp to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since 2023. Diesel set another AAA record at $6.31.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. That act is now finished in practice: ~3 mb of contracted barrels remain and the weekly draw has fallen to 0.4 mb. The reserve has stopped moving at precisely the moment the price broke $100.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

Realised lift averaged 0.72 mb/day against an advertised 1.43, peaking at ~1.31 in May, with no return barrels since February. The programme has now effectively stopped: −1.6 mb over two weeks to 285.0 mb, against −6.1 mb in a single week in early August, leaving roughly 3 mb of contracted barrels. No DOE statement calls the programme finished — the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on about half a million barrels, and the awarded book has simply run out. But on 14 September Energy Secretary Wright said refilling “will start in the next few months,” seeking some $20bn, which is the first official signal that the release is treated as over.

→ Implication · the suppression ended as arithmetic before it ended as policy. A refill bid would turn the reserve from a seller into a buyer in the tightest market of the episode.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
fired

Both premiums deepened again — Brent −$4.11 to −$5.07, Dubai −$12.28 to −$15.28 — with the front-month through $100 for the first time since July. The cause this week is physical rather than military: the Petroline bypass was shut on 11 September and the Red Sea route closed behind it, so prompt barrels lost their last route out of the Gulf. The premium eased only when Aramco gave a repair timetable on 16 September and then opened a ship-to-ship route off Sohar — moving crude out through Hormuz itself, the chokepoint the bypass existed to avoid.

→ Implication · the curve is pricing routes, not just the chokepoint. Watch the Petroline repair and the Sohar transfer volumes, not the diplomacy.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
strengthened

The conflict has outlasted the programme outright. With ~3 mb of contracted barrels the reserve can no longer influence price, while the IEA has cut 2026 world supply by 5.7 mb/day, the bypass is shut and the corridor was never opened. Weeks-to-floor now reads 71–104 — not because the buffer grew, but because the draw stopped.

→ Implication · read the longer runway as exhaustion, not recovery. A reserve that has stopped drawing because it has nothing left to draw is not a buffer.

Three things in the DOE ledger. It reconciles — net monthly movements imply an end-2025 stock of 413.4 mb against the 413.46 mb the Bloomberg series prints, so the cumulative-drawn figure rests on a flow ledger rather than a difference of stocks. The return leg is empty — zero exchange-return barrels from March onward, on a loan with a repayment premium, which is what makes Wright’s refill remark consequential. And one number is unresolved — DOE’s legend defines a Release No. 3 that no month carries; if it sits outside the ~133.5 mb contracted, the ~3 mb residual understates what DOE could still deliver.

What this implies

The price has detached from DOE entirely. Crude rose 5.4% in the week the US destroyed eight Iranian tankers and Iran extended its exclusion zone into the Arabian Sea, while the reserve did not print at all. Headline exposure should be sized as though the buffer were absent, because as a price-setting instrument it is. Barrels are moving; Iranian barrels are not. Aramco resumed VLCC loadings inside the strait and Murban holds a ~$7 premium to Brent, while China’s imports of Iranian crude nearly halved to 534 kb/d. The structural response is now funded — TotalEnergies backed an expansion of the Habshan–Fujairah bypass to 1.8 mb/d — but the immediate answer to the Yanbu outage is cruder: Aramco is shuttling barrels out through Hormuz via ship-to-ship transfers off Sohar, which trades pipeline risk for chokepoint risk.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

Two clocks have stopped, the corridor never started, and what is running now is a shooting war over tankers.

The US buffer clock is now a backstop, not a countdown. ~3 mb of contracted deliveries remain of the ~133.5 mb awarded, with no replacement since the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on about half a million barrels. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 85.0 mb below current stock — but at the current 0.4 mb weekly draw that is 71–104 weeks, against 19–28 a week ago. The runway lengthened because the programme stopped, not because the buffer grew. Two prints landed this edition, the Bloomberg feed carrying only the later one: −1.2 mb for the week to 4 September and −0.4 mb for the week to 11 September. The point is now beyond argument: Brent broke $100 in the week the reserve drew four hundred thousand barrels. Whatever moves this market, it is not the SPR.

Which floor? Three numbers circulate. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here — now ~37 mb above current stock. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability at 61% of design before ~126 mb came out. The dispute is now public: Hochstein said on 15 August that going below 300 damages the caverns; DOE rebutted that “the caverns are always full — all that changes is the ratio of oil and water.” The reserve is already below 290.

The global buffer is worse than the American one. The IEA's 11 September report cut 2026 world supply by 5.7 mb/day and demand by 2.5, with Gulf diesel and gasoil net exports at just over a quarter of pre-war levels. The US SPR's 130.5 mb of cumulative draw is a large share of the world's destocking to date — this paper's thesis restated in someone else's data — and the balance is still being closed by demand destruction rather than by barrels.

The routes closed, then partly reopened. Drones from Iraqi territory struck the Saudi East–West (Petroline) pipeline on 10–11 September and Riyadh shut it on the 11th; it is the only large-scale Hormuz bypass, and no Saudi crude has departed Yanbu since. The Houthis took Mocha on the 10th and Dhubab, the Hanish islands and Perim on the 11th — but by 14 September they appeared to have overextended, with no sign of a continuing presence on Perim and government forces counter-attacking, so Bab el-Mandeb is contested rather than held. Then the workaround arrived: Aramco said on the 16th it aims to restore half of Petroline within days and full capacity in roughly six weeks, and on the 17th began offering crude to Asian refiners via ship-to-ship transfers off Oman’s Sohar — shuttling barrels out through the strait the pipeline existed to avoid. Rapidan puts the net September loss near 400 kb/d. That sequence, not the diplomacy, took the top off the price.

The Iranian clock — rerouting, but not replacing. Kharg Island reactivated on 12 August after a 25-day halt and Caspian shipments from Russia have roughly tripled since the blockade began, but the volume has not followed: China’s imports of Iranian crude fell to 534 kb/d in August from 823 in July against a 1.4 mb/d 2025 average. The rial hit a record 2.02 m to the dollar on 24 August. Iran is rerouting around the blockade; the reroute is not replacing the barrels — which is the context for blacklisting 45–46 Gulf tankers and threatening confiscation.

Deeper records in both basins with the front-month through $100, driven now by the loss of the bypass and the Red Sea route rather than by the chokepoint alone. The SPR has stopped drawing with ~3 mb contracted left, so the dislocation is landing into a market with neither cushion nor hedge — the configuration the paper has described since v2.

Products run on a separate clock underneath. On the Bloomberg series US average gasoline printed $5.20 on 15 September — but that series tracks roughly $0.83 above AAA’s regular-unleaded average of $4.37, which remains below its June 2022 record; it sits close to AAA premium at $5.26. The record is again diesel, at $6.31 (16 Sep, an AAA all-time high and up 37 cents in a week). AAA logged the largest one-week jump in the national average of the episode. On 16 September the Federal Reserve raised rates 25bp to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since 2023 — a demand-side headwind now pulling against the supply story. Refinery utilisation ran 97.4% in the week to 21 August, not the ~92% carried before v2.11, which makes the picture tighter on demand and looser on spare capacity than the paper had been saying. Energy-led inflation peaked at 4.2% y/y in May and eased to 3.4% in July — a lag that means no Hormuz outcome reaches the pump on the timeline the politics assumes. Product stocks are the sharper stress: gasoline drew 2.5 mb in the week to 21 August to ~14% below the five-year average, even as distillate built 2.2 mb. If a dislocation arrives, it is likelier to present as a diesel crisis than a crude one.

What this implies

What remains live is the physical reopening clock — mines, escorts, insurers, ~600 stranded ships. That is the variable to watch, and transit counts read it more honestly than headlines do.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

The test this paper set has now failed twice in a row, and the reason it failed is itself the finding.

The suppression thesis has played out in full. The draw ran to a 43-year low and through it, the corridor never opened, and with ~3 mb of contracted barrels the reserve is spent. The central prediction is realised in the order specified: the barrels ran out, the mask came off at the closed strait first and the Atlantic second, and tightness surfaced in structure as the buffer emptied rather than after. The open question is no longer whether the suppression ends but how far the risk premium travels now that it is no longer anchored to a single waterway.

Soft branch recovering

  • A signed text with maritime control resolved and mines clearing on schedule
  • Absent no basis narrowing, no transit count published, and no sign of a text

Hard branch winning

  • Present corridor never opened and the bypass is shut — though a Sohar ship-to-ship route has partly substituted
  • Present deeper records — Brent −$5.07, Dubai −$15.28 — on lost routes, not just the chokepoint
  • Present outright through $100 — a $103.31 peak on 15 Sep, easing on the workaround
  • Exhausted SPR draw down to 0.4 mb/week with ~3 mb contracted left — the programme has stopped

Exhaustion risk — realised, and the thesis closes here. The paper has argued since v1 that the barrels and the signal are spent in the same act, and that when the barrels run out the signal dies with them. That is now observable: the draw has fallen to 0.4 mb in a week with ~3 mb of contracted volume left, and in the same week Brent broke $100 without the reserve exerting any visible influence on it. The restated test — a basis narrowing while the outright falls, alongside transits in the dozens — failed again, and this time on every leg: both bases deepened, the outright rose, and PortWatch counted eight transits on 13 September against a pre-crisis baseline near 85.

Positioning risk — now a routing problem. A curve turned at the front but anchored at the back was the classic pre-dislocation shape; this week the front ran further still, both bases printing deeper records while the outright cleared $100. What has changed is the nature of the risk being priced: with Petroline shut and Bab el-Mandeb contested, the market is pricing the loss of routes, not the closure of one strait. A hedge built around Hormuz reopening does not cover a pipeline outage, and the Aramco repair timetable has become the single most price-sensitive variable in the complex.

Optical-deal risk — overtaken. The corridor never opened and has been overtaken twice over: first by direct fire, now by the loss of the bypass. Reported US talks with Houthi representatives in Oman on 12–13 September are the only live diplomatic thread, and they concern the Red Sea rather than Hormuz.

Escalation risk. The tanker war continues — two sailors were reported missing after a strike in the strait on 15 September — but the week’s decisive attacks were on infrastructure, launched from Iraqi territory, which Washington attributed to Iran-backed proxies and Tehran categorically denied. Iraq’s 30 September militia-disarmament deadline now sits directly on the price. Discount definitive claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The asymmetry runs the other way from March. Then the reserve capped the upside and the risk was paying for a premium DOE could suppress. Now the front is bid, the long end is not, and there is no instrument left to flatten a dislocation. Whatever the directional view, the distribution has fattened on one side only.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.
