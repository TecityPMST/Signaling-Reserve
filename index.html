What changed — week to 20 August
  • The reserve drew again. First print since the deadline lapsed: −5.3 mb to 293.4 mb (14 Aug), a fresh series low at 0.75 mb/day. Cumulative 122.0 mb; ~11.5 mb contracted left; cushion 93.4 mb.
  • The memorandum expired 17 August with the nuclear and sanctions files never opened. Baghaei called the deadline “entirely moot”; Trump threatened to bomb Oman, the mediator, and now says no talks are under way.
  • A Gulf state broke with Tehran. The UAE suspended financial and economic transactions with Iran after accusing it of firing ballistic missiles at Emirati territory — the first Gulf economic rupture of the war.
  • The waterway got more dangerous. A crew member killed 18 August, three China-linked supertankers turned back mid-transit, eight vessel attacks this month. Kpler counted 3, 2, 9 and 6 transits on 15–18 August against a ten-day average of 11. Trump maintains the strait is open.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. That act is complete, and the diplomacy it bought time for has formally run out — yet the barrels are still leaving.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

Realised lift averaged 0.79 mb/day across the programme against an advertised 1.43, peaking at ~1.31 in the three record weeks of May. DOE's own movement ledger confirms 116.6 mb released March–August against zero return barrels since February. The first print since the deadline lapsed shows the reserve drew a further −5.3 mb to 293.4 mb in the week to 14 August — 0.75 mb/day, in line with the programme average and entirely unaffected by the collapse of the diplomacy it was funding.

→ Implication · the programme is no longer buying time for anything. The floor, not the diplomacy, sets the end date.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
weakening

Both bases remain negative and have compressed off their August extremes — Brent −$2.16 to −$1.83, Dubai −$3.75 to −$2.33 — but not because the prompt eased. The front rose $2.97 to $89.79 while spot rose $2.64: the back of the curve is catching up. On 20 August the Brent basis widened again to −$1.93 with the outright at $90.08, a fourth consecutive session higher. Physical corroborates: Murban has swung from a discount to Brent in early August to a ~$7 premium, the widest since April.

→ Implication · read this as the long end repricing failure, not as the prompt premium resolving. The under-hedged shape this paper has flagged since v2 is starting to correct.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
strengthened

The 60-day memorandum expired on 17 August with the talks never having begun, and Trump has since said there are none ongoing. The programme has now outlasted the diplomatic window it was sized against — with ~11.5 mb of contracted barrels left, 93.4 mb of cushion above the floor, 18–21 weeks of runway and no tender since June.

→ Implication · the buffer is the only instrument still standing, and it is a backstop rather than a countdown. There is nothing behind it.

Three things in the DOE ledger. It reconciles — net monthly movements imply an end-2025 stock of 413.4 mb against the 413.46 mb the Bloomberg series prints, and the March–August release total of 116.6 mb matched the peak-to-date fall as at the 7 August statement. The two streams share an originator, so this is an internal-consistency check, but the cumulative-drawn figure now rests on a flow ledger rather than a difference of stocks. The return leg is empty — zero exchange-return barrels every month since February, on a loan with a repayment premium, and the GAO's finding that the reserve accepts returning crude at only 56% of specification applies to a repayment leg that is entirely prospective. And one number is unresolved — DOE's legend defines a Release No. 3 that no month carries. If it sits outside the ~133.5 mb contracted, the ~11.5 mb residual understates what DOE could deliver.

What this implies

The price has detached from DOE entirely. The August path — −8% on a called-off strike, +11.5% on a conditioned reopening, then four straight sessions higher into the memorandum’s expiry — owes nothing to a reserve that drew 5.3 mb over the same stretch. Headline exposure should be sized as though the buffer were absent, because as a price-setting instrument it is. Two supply-side developments cut against a simple closure reading: Saudi Aramco resumed VLCC loadings from inside the strait in the week of 12–16 August, three ships lifting ~2 mb each after a three-week gap, and ADNOC cut spot offers to Asia ~5% while Murban ran to a four-month high of $97.75, a ~$7 premium to Brent and the widest since April. Barrels are moving again; they are simply moving expensively.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

Two clocks have stopped, one expired on 17 August, and the only one still running is physical.

The US buffer clock is now a backstop, not a countdown. ~11.5 mb of contracted deliveries remain of the ~133.5 mb awarded, with no replacement since the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on 0.5, and no new tender in the week to 20 August. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 93.4 mb below current stock — 18–21 weeks depending on the pace window. Total US crude, commercial plus SPR, stands at 722.2 mb, with commercial crude building 4.4 mb to 428.8 mb in the week to 14 August — back at its five-year average even as the strategic reserve makes a new low. August has shown this clock no longer sets the price: crude fell ~8% on a called-off strike, rose 11.5% when the Oman route arrived conditioned rather than free, and ran four straight sessions higher into the memorandum's expiry — none of it on DOE, which drew 5.3 mb over the same stretch without moving the tape.

Which floor? Three numbers circulate. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here — a political marker, now ~41 mb above current stock. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb, which would triple the notional cushion. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability already at 61% of design before ~122 mb came out. Readers preferring DOE's number should read the cushion as ~223 mb, then ask how fast any of it could move. The dispute went public this week. Amos Hochstein said on 15 August that “I don't know anyone who believes we can go below 300 — physically you will damage the caverns,” a view a Texas A&M petroleum engineer endorsed; DOE's chief spokesman rebutted on the record two days later that “the caverns are always full — all that changes is the ratio of oil and water.” The reserve is already below 300. The paper's ~200 mb operational floor sits between the two positions, and the fact that the question is now being argued in public rather than settled internally is itself evidence that no one outside DOE knows the answer.

The global buffer is worse than the American one. The IEA's August report puts observed global inventories down 69 mb in July to below 7.9 bn barrels and 410 mb since the war began, ~2.7 mb/day. The US SPR's 122 mb is roughly 30% of that — one national reserve accounting for nearly a third of world destocking, which is this paper's thesis restated in someone else's data. The agency sees a 1.8 mb/day Q3 deficit, closed by cutting demand rather than finding barrels.

The diplomatic clock has expired. The 60-day memorandum signed on 19 June lapsed on 17 August with the nuclear and sanctions talks never having begun. Its vague drafting was the proximate cause — above all on who controls transit — and Iran struck several ships that failed to follow its approved route. Tehran declared the deadline moot on the ground of US violations; Washington dismissed it equally. Trump then threatened to bomb Oman, the mediator of the only functioning track, and has since said no talks are under way at all. The UAE suspended financial and economic transactions with Iran after accusing it of firing ballistic missiles at Emirati territory — the first Gulf economic rupture of the war, removing another mediating constituency. The paper has argued since v2 that the contested deadline and the buffer were synchronised to the same window. That window has closed with the buffer nearly spent and nothing agreed.

The Iranian clock has restarted — a correction to last week's reading. Kharg Island, ~90% of Iranian crude exports, reactivated its western terminal on 12 August after a 25-day halt: a 333-metre VLCC berthed and loaded, the first since 18 July, with a second NITC VLCC lifting ~2 mb at the Azarpad jetty. The eastern terminal and the LPG facility remain idle and 16–17 tankers still wait offshore, so this is partial resumption, not restoration. Two other data points cut the same way: the North Larak dark-fleet anchorage grew to 35 hulls by 12 August, and Caspian wet-cargo shipments from Russia have risen roughly threefold since the blockade began. Tehran also booked $7.5bn in oil revenue in the first four months of 2026, up ~50% year on year on Fars' telling, price offsetting volume. The revenue clock is therefore not running to zero as this paper argued a week ago — Iran is rerouting around the blockade rather than waiting for it to lift, which removes a source of pressure the paper had been counting on.

Prompt premium at the strait since the 1 July roll, Brent joining in August, the outright following rather than leading. Triggered by diplomatic breakdown rather than SPR exhaustion — but the clocks have converged, so the next dislocation lands into a market with neither cushion nor hedge.

Products run on a separate clock underneath. Gasoline is $4.79 (18 Aug), grinding higher rather than tracking the crude round-trip, and Trump has begun asking Americans to accept higher pump prices. Refinery utilisation is running ~96–97% — outlets differ on the exact print for the week to 14 August — not the ~92% carried before v2.11, which makes the picture tighter on demand and looser on spare capacity than the paper had been saying.11, making the picture tighter on demand and looser on spare capacity than the paper had said. Energy-led inflation peaked at 4.2% y/y in May and eased to 3.4% in July — a lag that means no Hormuz outcome reaches the pump on the timeline the politics assumes. The sharper stress is in distillate: stocks fell a further 1.5 mb in the week to 14 August and sit ~13% below the five-year average, with US diesel margins at records. If a dislocation arrives, it is likelier to present as a diesel crisis than a crude one.

What this implies

What remains live is the physical reopening clock — mines, escorts, insurers, ~600 stranded ships. That is the variable to watch, and transit counts read it more honestly than headlines do.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

The falsification test set in v2.10 fired on both legs inside one week. This is what replaces it.

The suppression thesis has substantially played out. The draw ran to a 43-year low and through it, the interim deal collapsed, and with ~11.5 mb of contracted barrels left the reserve is effectively spent. The central prediction is realised in the order specified: the barrels ran out, the mask came off at the closed strait first and the Atlantic second, and tightness surfaced in structure as the buffer emptied rather than after. The open question is no longer whether the suppression ends, but whether the market is under-pricing a strait that stays physically shut while the long end prices comfort.

Soft branch recovering

  • A signed text with maritime control resolved and mines clearing on schedule
  • Backwardation compressing with the outright falling — prompt easing, not the back catching up

Hard branch winning

  • Present the 60-day memorandum expired 17 Aug with talks never begun; mediator threatened
  • Present both basins backwardated; compression this week came from the back rising, not the prompt easing
  • Present outright turning up with the structure — $89.92 on 19 Aug, a four-week high
  • Present SPR still drawing after the deadline lapsed — −5.3 mb to a fresh low

Exhaustion risk — still primary, and the test needs restating. Earlier versions treated compressing backwardation as the soft branch returning. That was wrong as written: compression has arrived with a lapsed memorandum, attacked ships and vessels turning back. The signal is not the spread narrowing but which leg narrows it. Compression from a falling front is the soft branch; compression from a rising back, as now, is the market extending the disruption in time. Restated: only a basis narrowing while the outright falls, alongside transits sustained in the dozens, returns the soft branch.

Positioning risk — beginning to correct. A curve turned at the front but anchored at the back is the classic pre-dislocation shape. The back has now begun to move: both bases compressed because the front outran spot, not because the prompt eased. That is the market pricing a failure it had treated as temporary. The correction is early — the long end still sits well below the prompt — but a market repricing the back while the strait stays shut is repricing the disruption’s duration, not its severity.

Optical-deal risk, now remote. With the memorandum lapsed and the mediator threatened, a signed text that headline-reopens Hormuz is a less likely near-term path than a week ago. If one arrives, the detection signal is unchanged: the Dubai basis fails to normalise and transits stay thin.

Escalation risk, now the base case for the tape. Combat has widened to the shipping lane and to the Gulf's own politics — ADNOC vessels struck, a seafarer killed, the mediator threatened with bombing, the UAE cutting financial ties after missiles landed on its territory, and Iran warning Gulf states that facilitation makes them belligerents. Discount definitive “strait open” claims until transit counts confirm them; that assertion was made on a day a crew member died in the waterway.

What this implies

The asymmetry runs the other way from March. Then the reserve capped the upside and the risk was paying for a premium DOE could suppress. Now the front is bid, the long end is not, and there is no instrument left to flatten a dislocation. Whatever the directional view, the distribution has fattened on one side only.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.

05Method, verification and sources

Conventions, the caveats a reader must carry, and this week's verification only. Earlier weeks' ledgers stay in the version files they were published in.

Conventions. Prices, spreads, SPR levels and gasoline come only from the Tecity Bloomberg pull; news and context are web-sourced and separately verified. Brent front-month = CO1; basis = front minus spot, so positive is contango and negative backwardation. Dubai = DBL1−DBLA. SPR = DOESSPR. Operational floor = the ~200 mb Tecity estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb conventionally cited; cushion = level minus 200. The weekly point is the most recent session with a complete print in both legs — 19 August this week, since 20 August carries Brent but no Dubai print. Stages 1–6 of the argument are in v2.11 §02.
