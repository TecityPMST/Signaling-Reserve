What changed — week to 1 October
  • DOE reloaded the instrument. On 29 September the Energy Department solicited an exchange of up to 40 mb from Big Hill and Bryan Mound, delivery November–December, bids due 6 October, barrels returned with a premium DOE puts at ~25%. It is the sixth solicitation under the 172 mb US share of the 400 mb IEA action. This paper called the programme finished a week ago; it is not.
  • The Gulf rerouted back to pre-war volumes without the strait. At least 16.5 mb/day left the region in September, matching the pre-war average excluding Iran, but only 60% crossed Hormuz against 83% before the war — 23% now loads at Fujairah and 17% exits via the Red Sea, moved by a shuttle fleet of 63 VLCCs.
  • The negotiation moved the price, twice. Trump rejected Tehran’s seven-day reopening plan on the 26th; spot Brent jumped ~3% to about $108 on the 28th. A US counterproposal reached Tehran on the 30th, with sequencing the stated sticking point, and the front month closed the week at $96.61, −3.6%.
  • The prompt premium set a record, then collapsed. Brent’s basis hit −$7.45 on 28 September — the deepest of the episode, on the rejected plan and three to four tankers struck in the strait on the 28th–29th — and was back to −$3.31 by 1 October, the narrowest since 1 September. Dubai eased to −$13.77 from a −$17.89 record.
  • The draw re-accelerated as the book ran out. SPR 283.77 mb (25 Sep), −0.79 on the week against −0.41 and −0.40 before it, leaving ~1.8 mb of the awarded book. Restated: the 24 September close settles at $100.22, not the $97.40 v3.4 carried from an intraday pull.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. The correction this week is to the timing, not the mechanism: the awarded book is down to ~1.8 mb, and DOE has gone back for up to 40 mb more. The instrument was reloaded rather than retired — into a market that had already solved the supply problem by rerouting, which is the clearest evidence yet that what is being managed is the price, not the barrels.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
strengthened

The new solicitation makes the point better than the old arithmetic did. Up to 40 mb over November and December is ~0.65 mb/day — against a 9–13 mb/day shortfall, and into a Gulf that had already restored pre-war export volumes by rerouting through Fujairah and the Red Sea. Supply is not what is short. Realised lift to date is 0.67 mb/day over 196 days against an advertised 1.43, with no return barrels since February. The June tender for 40 mb drew bids on about half a million barrels; the same 40 mb is now offered into a market with more barrels and lower freight risk than then.

→ Implication · a release announced after the physical problem was solved cannot be a supply remedy. Watch the 6 October bid count — it prices how much of this is signal.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
contested

The basis did two things the reserve cannot explain. It set a record −$7.45 on 28 September, as Trump’s rejection of Tehran’s plan met three to four tankers struck in the strait on the 28th and 29th, and collapsed to −$3.31 by 1 October, when the counterproposal landed — the narrowest since 1 September, a two-thirds retracement in four sessions. Dubai eased from its −$17.89 record to −$13.77. The DOE solicitation, announced on the 29th in the middle of that move, is not visible in it at all.

→ Implication · the prompt premium is now a negotiation instrument, not an inventory one. It will keep trading the sequencing dispute, and no release of this size will anchor it.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
contested

Stated plainly: this claim took a hit this week. The awarded book is down to ~1.8 mb, but the programme did not stop at exhaustion — DOE reloaded it. What survives is the arithmetic: a full 40 mb exchange would take the reserve to about 243.8 mb, leaving 43.8 mb above the house floor and putting it 8.6 mb below the 252.4 mb EPCA statutory reference for the first time. Room is being bought by lowering the floor the reserve runs at, not by ending the conflict.

→ Implication · the constraint moved from the contracted book to the cavern. If the 40 mb clears, the next binding limit is statutory and physical, not commercial.

What this implies

The sequence this week is the paper’s argument in miniature. Exports returned to pre-war volumes by rerouting; the strait stayed mostly shut; the price moved on a rejected plan and a counterproposal; and the reserve’s contribution was to announce another 40 mb for delivery after all of it. A release timed after the physical problem was solved and during a negotiation is a price instrument by construction. Size the exposure to the 6 October bid count and the sequencing dispute, not to the headline volume — the June tender showed what this market does with 40 mb it does not need.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock restarted, the route clock has largely run its course, and the diplomatic clock is now the only one that moves the price.

The US buffer clock restarted this week. The awarded book is down to ~1.8 mb, but on 29 September DOE solicited up to 40 mb more — exchange, not sale, from Big Hill and Bryan Mound, delivery November and December, bids due 6 October, returns at a premium DOE puts at ~25%. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 83.8 mb below current stock; at the last three weekly prints (−0.40, −0.41, −0.79 mb) that is 107–158 weeks, against 68–124 a week ago on the same method — a number that becomes meaningless if the 40 mb clears, because the solicitation alone is 48 weeks of draw at the current pace. Deliver it in full and the reserve sits near 243.8 mb: 43.8 mb above this paper’s floor, and 8.6 mb below the EPCA statutory reference.

Which floor? The question stops being academic if the exchange clears. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here — it sits ~31 mb below current stock and would be crossed by a full 40 mb delivery. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability at 61% of design before ~126 mb came out. Hochstein said on 15 August that going below 300 damages the caverns; DOE rebutted that “the caverns are always full — all that changes is the ratio of oil and water.”

The route clock has largely run. At least 16.5 mb/day of crude left the Mideast Gulf in September — the pre-war average excluding Iran — but the map underneath changed: 60% crossed Hormuz (9.9 mb/day) against 83% before the war, 23% loaded at Fujairah and 17% left through the Red Sea, moved by a shuttle fleet of 63 VLCCs with over 70% of August’s Hormuz crude changing tankers offshore. Saudi exports ran 5.1–5.6 mb/day in September against 2.4 in August. Transits through the strait themselves numbered 132 in the week to 27 September against roughly 130 a day before the war. The barrels found a way round; the waterway did not reopen.

The diplomatic clock is the one that moves the price. Tehran’s seven-day plan — reopen the strait and return to nuclear talks against released funds, lifted sanctions and an end to the naval blockade — was rejected by Trump on 26 September, and spot Brent jumped about 3% to roughly $108 on the 28th. A US counterproposal reached Tehran on the 30th, presented by Araghchi to Pezeshkian, with the sequencing of steps the stated obstacle; the front month gave back 3.6% on the week. Nothing is signed, and the Revolutionary Guard’s public position remains that Washington must withdraw from the region.

The Iranian clock — still rerouting, now also losing leverage. Iran’s own 1.7 mb/day of pre-war exports is the entire gap between September’s 16.5 and the full pre-war number, and it is blocked by US policy rather than by the strait. That is the uncomfortable symmetry of the week: the more completely the Gulf reroutes around Hormuz, the less the closure is worth as a bargaining chip — and the more the remaining premium rests on the blockade of Iran rather than on the waterway.

Soft
in play
The arrangement signs and delivers free passage. A plan, a rejection and a counterproposal inside six days is the most movement since June, and the basis traded every step of it. Still no text, and 132 transits a week is not a reopening.
Optical
the live risk
Headline reopening while physical flow lags. The risk has sharpened: with exports already at pre-war volumes by other routes, a “reopening” could be announced and change very little in the water — while a 40 mb release delivered into it would meet a market that no longer needs the barrels.
Hard
receding
Record prompt premiums with the outright above $100 — the standing condition since July, and for the first time it is easing on something other than a repair: Brent’s basis retraced two-thirds in four sessions and Dubai came off its record. The reserve played no part in either direction.

Products are still the tight leg, and still the policy question. Diesel came off its record to $6.45 on 28 September from $6.52, but the stock position deteriorated: distillate fell another 2.3 mb in the week to 25 September to 14% below the five-year average, from 12% a week earlier. Trump said he is “thinking very seriously” about an export ban while conceding it “can oftentimes lead to a little bit of an increase on gasoline”; the administration is working three options short of one — voluntary refiner curbs, a federal diesel-tax suspension, and state-level action already live in Nebraska and Alabama — and a White House official says no decision has been made. Russia extended its own diesel export ban to the end of October. On the Bloomberg series US average gasoline eased to $5.29 (29 Sep) from a $5.35 peak; that series runs ~$0.90 above AAA’s regular average. Crude is being solved by freight; diesel is not, and it is where the next intervention lands.

What this implies

Two dates now carry the price: 6 October, when the 40 mb bids are counted, and whatever sequencing the counterproposal settles. A thin bid book would say the market reads the release as signal; a full one would say 40 mb of cheap borrowed crude is worth taking whatever the strait does.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

One of this paper's three claims took a hit this week. It is recorded here rather than smoothed over.

The claim that the programme would run out of room before the conflict resolved assumed a finite awarded book. The book is nearly finished — ~1.8 mb — but DOE has reloaded it with up to 40 mb, so exhaustion arrives later and on different terms, and the paper marks that claim contested rather than carried. What the week confirms instead is the other two: a release announced after the Gulf had restored pre-war export volumes by rerouting cannot be a supply remedy, and a prompt premium that set an episode record on a rejected peace plan and retraced two-thirds on a counterproposal is not being set by inventory.

Soft branch recovering

  • Present exports at pre-war volumes; Brent basis retraced to −$3.31, narrowest since 1 Sep
  • Present a plan, a rejection and a US counterproposal inside six days
  • Absent no signed text; 132 transits in the week to 27 Sep against ~130 a day pre-war

Hard branch winning

  • Present episode-record Brent basis −$7.45 (28 Sep) on a rejected plan and tankers hit in the strait
  • Present diesel near records at $6.45 with distillate 14% below the five-year average, from 12%
  • Reloaded SPR book down to ~1.8 mb — and a 40 mb solicitation issued on 29 Sep

Exhaustion risk — deferred, not resolved. The barrels and the signal are still spent in the same act; what changed is that the act was repeated. The test is now dated: bids close 6 October. If the book fills, the reserve has bought roughly 48 weeks of current-pace draw and will cross the EPCA statutory reference on delivery. If it comes back thin, as June’s did at about half a million barrels against 40 mb offered, then the instrument is spent in a more final way than any inventory number shows — the market will have declined free barrels.

Positioning risk — the premium is political now. A basis that moves $4.14 in four sessions on a rejection and a counterproposal is pricing a negotiation, not a shortage. Hedges built on inventory or on chokepoint closure have both decoupled: the chokepoint is 40% bypassed and the inventory is being topped up by tender. The exposure that remains is to headlines and to sequencing.

Optical-deal risk — sharper than before. Because the Gulf already moves pre-war volumes without the strait, a reopening can now be announced with very little changing in the water, and the market has shown it will pay several dollars for the announcement. The correction mechanism is still the transit count, which at 132 a week says the strait is substantially shut.

Escalation risk. UKMTO logged three to four tankers struck by unknown projectiles on 28–29 September — among them the ADNOC-operated Mersin Prosperity and the Sinbad — with no casualties reported; Iran’s Fars called them warning shots and an IRGC spokesman said unpermitted vessels “either will be targeted or will hit a mine.” Seventeen vessels transited on the 29th regardless, which is the week in one line: the threat is live and decreasingly effective. The last US forces left Iraq on 30 September, ending a 23-year presence, on the same day Baghdad’s militia-disarmament deadline passed with no movement and the committee’s own date pushed to June 2027. The drones that took down Petroline came from Iraqi territory three weeks ago. Discount definitive claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The distribution has two fat tails now and they sit on different dates. A thin 6 October book plus a sequencing breakdown is the old hard branch with no instrument left; a full book plus a signed text is a market long freight and long borrowed crude into a reopening it has already priced.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.
