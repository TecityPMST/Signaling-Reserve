What changed — week to 12 November
  • The war was declared over, and the price finished giving back the premium. After meeting Qatar’s prime minister on 9 November, Trump said “Iran militarily is effectively over”, while adding that the US is “locked and loaded”. Brent settled −5.85% that session and ended the week at $89.61 on CO1, −6.31%, the lowest since 31 August. Nothing has been signed, Iran’s four conditions are unchanged, and Tehran publicly denies the talks exist.
  • The paper’s central number turned negative. CO1 now sits 13.1% below its level on 13 March, the day the release was announced. The 172 mb has been spent in full, and the price is lower than when the spending began — which is either the instrument working or the war ending, and the stock series cannot tell them apart.
  • The reserve did not report. EIA’s weekly was pushed to 12 November by the Veterans Day closure, so DOESSPR still ends 30 October at 282.18 mb and every SPR figure is carried from v3.10. A wire figure of 279.5 mb has no counterpart in the pull and is not used.
  • Bloomberg revised three sessions, and the revision corrects v3.10. The re-pulled file moves 6 November from CO1 96.42 / COA 100.12 to 95.65 / 98.32, and 5 November from 91.72 / 94.12 to 94.91 / 98.29. v3.10’s weekly point, its −13.63% week and its account of a Thursday low are all superseded. §04 records what this does to a rule written last week.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. Both are now spent. The release is complete, the war is declared over, and the price is below where the programme began — which is the outcome the instrument was built for and cannot prove it caused.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

The claim ends the programme in better shape than it began it. Realised lift was 0.58 mb/day over 231 days against an advertised 1.43 — the reserve never delivered at the rate it was announced at, and in the final eight weeks delivered almost nothing while the price moved by a fifth twice. What moved it both times was a sentence: a circulating draft on 2 November, a declaration of victory on the 9th. The instrument’s mechanism is confirmed and its ownership is not: the announcements that mattered were not DOE’s.

→ Implication · suppression by announcement is cheap and real, but it is rented, not owned. A reserve is what gives the announcement collateral; spending the reserve to make announcements credible is the trade this programme actually made.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread
retired

Retired this edition. The two benchmarks have converged on mild backwardation — Brent −2.05, Dubai −1.33 on 11 November — and neither leg now distinguishes a reserve effect from a war ending. The claim also lost its evidentiary base twice in three weeks: v3.9’s sour crossing was a roll artefact, and this week’s revision shows the Brent basis for 5 November was −3.38, not the −2.40 v3.10 plotted. A spread that cannot survive its own data revisions cannot carry a claim.

→ Implication · the spread stays in Fig. 2 as a description of market structure, not as evidence for this paper’s thesis. Anything the thesis asserts must now rest on the stock series and the award record.

The programme’s exposure is the return leg, not the floor
the live claim

Now the only one with anything left to test. DOE says 216 mb returns from early next year, including 44 mb of premium worth more than $4 billion — a 25.6% uplift on the 172 mb released, consistent with the 25% it cites on earlier exchanges. The return leg has booked zero barrels since the first quarter. And the economics have moved hard in the borrower’s favour: barrels taken at $110 can be replaced at $89.61, the lowest since August, which makes an in-kind return cheap to source and gives no one any reason to hurry.

→ Implication · with the war declared over, the only remaining test of the whole programme is whether 216 mb arrives. The first return print is the event this paper now exists to catch.

Everything the reserve has left to say about itself is in a column that has been empty for seven months.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

Three of the four clocks this paper has tracked have stopped. The one that is left runs to the return.

The buffer clock has stopped reporting. DOESSPR still ends at 282.18 mb for 30 October: the EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week to 6 November was deferred to 12 November by the Veterans Day closure, and the pull was taken that day. Level, cushion (82.2 mb), realised rate and weeks-to-floor (206 at the last draw, 309 at the three-week average) are all carried unchanged from v3.10. A figure of 279.5 mb described as the lowest since October 1982 circulated this week on one wire; it has no counterpart in the pull, it predates the deferred release, and it is not used — if it belongs to DOE’s own daily inventory rather than the EIA weekly series, the two are not comparable. Delivering the awarded 40 mb would still take the reserve to 242.2 mb, 10.2 mb below the 252.4 mb EPCA reference and 42.2 above the house floor.

The policy clock has stopped. Nothing was published in the window: no awardee names on the 40 mb exchange, no delivery schedule, no return dates, no new solicitation. The award release gave no dates, but the solicitation it answered did: the 29 September RFP states that “deliveries under awarded exchanges are scheduled for November and December 2026”. November is half gone and the series has not printed since 30 October. The last act was the 300,000-barrel heating oil award of 3 November, itself short of the 315,000 authorised and from two sites of three. A programme that issued four actions in eight days at the end of October has issued nothing in the nine days since.

The war clock has been declared stopped, without a signature. After meeting Qatari prime minister Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani at the White House on 9 November, Trump told a surprise briefing that “Iran militarily is effectively over”, that the US is “just keeping it the status quo”, and that military action “doesn’t seem to be a very necessary thing, but we’re locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason that changes”. He also said it was “going to be ending fairly soon” — not the same as over. Against that, no agreement has been signed, and Tehran disputes that negotiations exist at all. Security council secretary Mohsen Rezaei, the same day: “FOLD YOUR HAND. STOP manufacturing FAKE NEWS about imaginary talks.” Pezeshkian called it “neither war nor peace” and said Iran would not “surrender to force and bullying”; on the 12th Rezaei added that not “a single particle of enriched nuclear material” would leave Iran. The status quo Trump described explicitly includes the blockade — “a continuation of the greatest naval blockade in the history of our country” — and Task Force Iron Gate, stood up on 7 November to tighten it. Senior US and Gulf officials told Reuters they doubt an imminent deal. The market has priced a declaration that one party denies.

What is left is the return clock. It is the only one still running, and it has never ticked. DOE has committed to 216 mb back “beginning early next year”, including 44 mb of premium barrels it values at more than $4 billion. Against that commitment the record is seven months of nothing, a counterparty set DOE has never named, no published schedule, and a flat price that has fallen far enough to make repayment in kind cheap for whoever owes it. The reserve’s 2026 was spent in public; its 2027 will be settled in a column that is currently blank.

Soft

The President has called the war militarily over after meeting the mediator. Nothing is signed, no terms are published, and Iran’s preconditions are unchanged. The market has taken the declaration at face value and priced it.
Optical
exhausted
A formal reopening would now confirm a price move that has already happened twice. There is no optical value left to extract, which is itself a reason to expect the declaration to stand.
Hard
stood down, not dismantled
“Quite unlikely” is not a withdrawal. The blockade and the task force remain, Iran warns of consequences, and the strikes of early November were deliberately unpublicised — so a resumption would not necessarily be visible before it moved the price.

Products, and the one number that fell further than crude. On the Bloomberg series US average retail gasoline printed $4.86 on 10 November, down from the $5.35 peak of 24 September. It has closed below $5 on every session since 5 November, having last done so in early September. That is the number the election turned on, and it has now given back the war premium faster than the crude it is made from.

What this implies

Three questions carry the next edition: whether the SPR series resumes and at what level, whether a declaration without a signature survives contact with Iran’s preconditions, and whether any barrel comes back. Only the third is a test this paper can run unaided.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

A data revision this week corrects the last edition and overturns a rule this paper wrote seven days ago. That is recorded first.

Bloomberg revised three sessions, and the revision reverses a withdrawal. v3.10 first read the 5 November row as an incomplete capture — a $1.32 range and a 0.5% fall against wires reporting Brent up about 3.6% — and moved its weekly point to the 4th. It then withdrew that correction, because the 6 November pull carried 5 November unchanged, and wrote a rule from it: a price row is only provisional when the next pull revises it. The re-pulled file of 12 November now revises both sessions. 5 November becomes CO1 94.91 / COA 98.29, a rise of 3.0% and 3.9% rather than a fall; 6 November becomes 95.65 / 98.32. The original reading was right, the withdrawal was wrong, and the rule was too weak: two successive pulls carried the same bad values and only the third corrected them.

What corroborates the revision. Four reconciliations, all exact. COA for 6 November is now $98.32, the settle two outlets reported, and CLA $90.83, the WTI settle implied by Reuters’ arithmetic. For 9 November, COA $92.57 and CLA $86.85 match to the cent, and the one-day moves compute to −5.85% and −4.38% — the exact percentages Reuters printed, which the superseded values did not produce. Standing rule, replacing last week’s: a price row is provisional until a later pull confirms it and it reconciles with an independent settle; where the two disagree, the paper reports the disagreement rather than choosing.

Return risk — the only live exposure. The flat price has fallen to $89.61, the lowest since 31 August and 24% below the 31 March peak on the daily series. A counterparty that drew barrels when Brent was above $110 can replace them for roughly four-fifths of that, which makes the exchange cheap to settle in kind and removes any urgency to settle early. The premium DOE booked — 44 mb, more than $4 billion at the prices it cited — is worth materially less now, and DOE has published no valuation date. Nothing shows in the stock series until a return print appears.

Measurement risk — the columns are confirmed and the archive is not. The revision added two more exact COA matches and two for CLA, taking COA to nine wire prints matched and CLA to three, against none for CO1 and CL1. On the revised data the two columns now disagree about the programme’s outcome: against 13 March, CO1 is 13.1% lower and COA is 0.6% higher. The paper’s headline uses CO1 for continuity with v1–v3.10 and states the COA figure alongside it, because at this point the choice of column is the difference between a programme that ended with the price down and one that ended flat.

What this implies

The week's methodological finding is that this paper's confirmation rule was not strong enough, and the week's substantive finding is that its central claim now depends on which of two columns is read. Both point the same way: the archive needs restating onto COA before the programme can be scored.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking was defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow, and the gap held: 172 million barrels were declared spent, the price is below where the spending started, and almost nothing moved in the final eight weeks. Whether that is a victory for the instrument or merely the war ending on its own schedule cannot be settled from stock data, and this paper will not pretend otherwise. What can be settled is the other direction. 216 million barrels are owed. The column is empty.
