What changed — week to 8 October
  • The bids closed; the award has not appeared. The 40 mb exchange solicitation closed on 6 October. As of 8 October DOE has published no award and no bid count. The awarded book is down to ~1.0 mb: SPR 282.98 mb (2 Oct), −0.78 on the week, the same pace as the week before to within a thousand barrels.
  • The coordinated release turned to diesel. On 2 October the G7 set a four-month deadline for the last ~100 mb committed in March — not new barrels — with diesel front-loaded into the first 20 days; on 7 October IEA members backed finishing it fast, diesel first. Washington dropped the export-ban idea and on 5 October put tax-deferred red-dyed diesel on the highways.
  • The rerouting held, but its second route is now a front. Kpler puts the Gulf’s seven-day crude exports at 18.5 mb/day on 1 October, above the ~18 pre-war average; Shell puts regional flows at ~80%. Yemen’s government opened a Bab al-Mandeb offensive on 4–5 October, and Houthi strikes on Saudi airports killed three on 6–7 October.
  • Tehran hardened and ships were struck. Ghalibaf said on the 4th that Hormuz stays closed until Iran’s seven conditions are met; Pezeshkian called talks “meaningless” on the 5th. At least six ships were hit between 3 and 7 October, among them the On Peace, with 12 crew injured, and a tanker off Qatar on the 7th.
  • The prompt premium narrowed through all of it. Brent’s basis reached −$2.75 on 7 October, the narrowest weekly point since August and 63% back from the −$7.45 record of 28 September. The front month closed at $98.24 on the 8th, +1.7%. Dubai printed −$6.46, but most of its move spans the October roll.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. This week the coordinated action said its target out loud: the G7 and IEA members agreed to prioritise diesel in the remaining ~100 mb, the product whose price is still rising, while Gulf crude exports touched pre-war volumes in late September. A release redirected to the barrel with the worst price, not the barrel in shortest supply, is a price instrument.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

The coordinated action has now said its target is diesel. The G7 front-loads diesel into the first 20 days of its four-month deadline; IEA members agreed to prioritise diesel where possible; Washington’s own step was to put red-dyed diesel on the highways. Crude is not what is short. Kpler has Gulf crude exports above the pre-war average on four of the last seven September sessions. Realised SPR lift to date is 0.65 mb/day over 203 days, against an advertised 1.43, with no return barrels since the first quarter.

→ Implication · the instrument has followed the price, from crude to diesel. If it works, it should show up in the diesel price and product stocks, not in Brent.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
contested

Brent’s basis narrowed to −$2.75 on 7 October, the tightest weekly point since August, and has held between −$2.74 and −$3.54 at every session since 1 October. It narrowed while at least six ships were struck in and near Hormuz and fighting reached the Bab al-Mandeb. It also narrowed while the 40 mb bids closed and the G7 put a deadline on the last ~100 mb of the March stocks. This paper cannot separate those two explanations from the release side. What it can say is that the premium stopped responding to attacks on ships, and that this is easier to explain with Gulf exports near pre-war volumes than with a reserve whose awarded book is ~1.0 mb.

→ Implication · if the basis keeps narrowing with no SPR award and an empty book, the premium is pricing flow, not reserves. That would undercut the suppression reading of the spread, not support it.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
contested

The awarded book is down to ~1.0 mb, and the dated test this paper set — the 6 October bid count — has not reported. Room keeps being made by policy: DOE’s 40 mb offer sits inside the March commitment whose last ~100 mb the G7 now wants out within four months. Over the same week the conflict added a second contested chokepoint rather than resolving the first. A full 40 mb exchange would take the reserve to about 243.0 mb, leaving 43.0 mb above the house floor and 9.4 mb below the 252.4 mb EPCA statutory reference.

→ Implication · the constraint is still the cavern and the statute, not the contract book. An award would show where the market prices that constraint, and silence would say something about it too.

What this implies

The week moved the policy from crude to diesel, and the crude premium narrowed regardless. The G7 front-loaded diesel, the IEA prioritised it and Washington chose a diesel tax measure. Meanwhile the Brent basis tightened through a week of ship strikes, with Gulf exports near pre-war volumes. If the crude premium is easing on flows and the policy is chasing a product price, the reserve’s crude barrels are the least relevant part of the response. Watch diesel, and the 40 mb award, not the headline release volume.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock is waiting on an award, the policy clock has turned to diesel, and the route clock now runs through two contested straits.

The US buffer clock is waiting on an award. The awarded book is down to ~1.0 mb. Bids on the up-to-40 mb exchange — Big Hill and Bryan Mound, delivery November and December — closed on 6 October, and DOE has published neither an award nor a bid count. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 83.0 mb below current stock. At the last three weekly prints (−0.41, −0.79, −0.78 mb) that is 106–126 weeks, against 107–158 a week ago on the same method; the range narrowed because a slow mid-September week dropped out of the window. A full 40 mb award is ~51 weeks of draw at the current pace. Delivered, it would leave the reserve near 243.0 mb: 43.0 mb above this paper’s floor and 9.4 mb below the EPCA statutory reference.

Which floor? The question stops being academic if the exchange clears. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here. It sits ~31 mb below current stock and would be crossed by a full 40 mb delivery. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. Industry does not agree either: in the Dallas Fed’s third-quarter survey, 31% of executives put the SPR’s absolute minimum inventory level at 100–150 mb and 19% at 250–300 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability at 61% of design before ~126 mb came out.

The policy clock has turned to diesel. On 2 October Birol told G7 leaders that ~325 mb of the 400 mb March action had been released. The G7 set a four-month deadline for the last ~100 mb of those March commitments — the remainder, not new barrels — with a substantial diesel release front-loaded into the first 20 days, and pledged to refrain from energy export restrictions among members. On 7 October IEA members backed completing it as soon as possible, prioritising diesel to the extent possible, noting ~1.1 bn barrels of public stocks still held, over 200 mb of it diesel; the Governing Board reviews next week. That pledge cuts against the US export-ban option discussed last week, which Trump said on 2 October he would not pursue. Washington’s actual step, on 5 October, was an order allowing off-road red-dyed diesel on highways, with federal excise deferred to year-end.

The route clock now runs through two straits. Kpler’s provisional data put Gulf crude exports above pre-war on four of the last seven September sessions, with a seven-day average of 18.5 mb/day on 1 October against ~18 pre-war, and ~40% of September’s crude bypassing Hormuz. Kpler’s own September figure is 16.5 mb/day, so pre-war was touched on days, not across the month. Shell’s Sawan, on 6 October, put Middle East oil flows at ~80% of pre-war — a different measure, and a range this paper carries rather than resolves. The bypass relies on the Red Sea for ~17% of Gulf crude, and the Red Sea is now a front. Yemen’s government opened an offensive on 4–5 October to retake the Houthi-held Bab al-Mandeb coast, backed by a Saudi-led air campaign. Saudi aviation authorities confirmed three injured at Jazan and Najran on 5 October, then three killed and 36 injured at Abha and Riyadh’s King Khalid airport on 6–7 October. The Houthi claim on Aramco’s Rabigh refinery is unconfirmed.

The diplomatic clock stalled. Ghalibaf said on 4 October that the strait “will not be opened until our seven conditions, based on the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding” are met. Pezeshkian said on the 5th that talks have “no meaning” with an enemy that sanctions and threatens daily. Tehran’s foreign ministry says it will send further points on the US counterproposal through Qatar, describing the US text as nuclear-focused while Iran’s focus is the strait. Vance told Reuters on 6 October that Iran should make a “meaningful” cut in enrichment rather than give it up; an Iranian official said US ideas remain “at odds” with Tehran’s demands. Trump said the war will end “very soon” and argued that pump prices now reflect refinery problems rather than Hormuz. Nothing is signed.

Products are the tight leg, and now the policy target. EIA put distillate stocks at 105.1 mb in the week to 2 October, still well below the five-year average. On the Bloomberg series, US average gasoline eased to $5.21 (6 Oct) from $5.29 a week earlier and a $5.35 peak on 24 September. Crude is being handled by freight and rerouting. Diesel is not, and every lever pulled this week was aimed at it.

What this implies

Two dates now carry the price: the DOE award (or continued silence) on the 40 mb, and the IEA Governing Board next week, which sets the pace of the last ~100 mb and its diesel tranche. A thin or absent award would say the market reads the SPR release as signal. A fast diesel tranche would test whether stock releases can move a product price that crude flows have not.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

No claim changed status this week except the first, which moved from strengthened to holding. The dated test — the 6 October bid count — has not reported.

Last week this paper dated its exhaustion test to the 6 October bid deadline. The deadline passed, and by 8 October DOE had published no award. Silence is not evidence either way, and the paper does not treat it as a thin book. What the week did show is the coordinated action redirecting its remaining volume to diesel, while the crude prompt premium eased on its own. That fits the claim that the release targets price. It complicates the claim that suppression is visible in the crude spread, which stays contested.

Soft branch recovering

  • Present Brent basis −$2.75 (7 Oct), narrowest weekly point since August; Gulf crude exports above pre-war on Kpler’s measure
  • Present Iran drafting further points on the US counterproposal via Qatar
  • Absent no signed text; Ghalibaf ties reopening to all seven conditions

Hard branch winning

  • Present at least six ships struck 3–7 Oct; 12 crew injured on the On Peace
  • Present war at the Bab al-Mandeb; 3 killed, 36 injured at Saudi airports (6–7 Oct)
  • Pending 40 mb bids closed, no award published; awarded book ~1.0 mb

Exhaustion risk — deferred again. The barrels and the signal are still spent in the same act. If the 40 mb is awarded in full, the reserve has bought about 51 weeks of current-pace draw and crosses the EPCA statutory reference on delivery. If no award appears, or a thin one does, as in June, when bids covered about half a million barrels of 40 mb offered, then the instrument is spent in a way no inventory number shows. The G7’s four-month deadline on the March remainder, which this offer is likely part of, sets the outer date either way.

Positioning risk — the crude premium has decoupled from incidents. A basis that tightens through six ship strikes in five days is pricing flow, not attacks. Hedges keyed to chokepoint headlines have stopped paying in crude. The exposure that remains sits in diesel, freight and the Red Sea leg of the bypass.

Optical-deal risk — still sharp. Because Gulf crude exports touched pre-war volumes in late September with ~40% bypassing the strait, a reopening can be announced with little changing in the water. The correction mechanism is still the transit count, and tracker counts are thin. Windward, a single tracker, logged 13 transits on 4 October, one of them dark.

Escalation risk — two chokepoints. UKMTO and shipping outlets logged the VLCCs Ghana Prosperity and Cameroon Prosperity, the LPG carrier Gas Vela, the LNG carrier Maran Gas Mystras and the On Peace hit between 3 and 6 October, and a tanker struck by multiple projectiles off Qatar on the 7th; there has been no formal claim. In Yemen, both sides claim the Bab al-Mandeb coast, and neither claim can yet be verified. A bypass that carries ~40% of Gulf crude now runs partly through an active front. Discount definitive claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The two fat tails are now geographic as well as dated. A Red Sea disruption would hit the bypass that let crude flows recover, and the SPR’s remaining crude could not replace it. A signed Hormuz text would meet a crude market that has already priced much of the reopening, and a diesel market that has not.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.
