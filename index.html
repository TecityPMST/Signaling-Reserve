What changed — week to 6 November
  • The price moved a fifth on sentences, in both directions. CO1 fell $111.63 to $91.72 by Thursday’s close, −17.84%, then rose 5.12% on Friday to $96.42 — a week of −13.63%, the steepest of the series against a previous worst of −12.66%. COA ran 115.75 to 94.12 to 100.12. Down: a draft to end the war (−8.9%, 2 Nov) and the midterm result (−8.7%, 4 Nov). Trump at the Lincoln Memorial on the 2nd: “That’s why I guess the oil prices dropped. People found out about it.” Up: Iran called the US reply to its Hormuz plan “not convincing”, the IRGC claimed six more tankers hit, and on the 5th Trump said he might “have to” destroy the rest of the country. Eurasia Group cut the odds of a deal from 20% to 10%.
  • The barrels did almost nothing, again. SPR 282.18 mb on 30 October, down 0.399 mb — four times the previous week, still only 0.057 mb/day. Against awards of more than 40 mb, the reserve delivered four tenths of one. Realised lift falls again, to 0.58 mb/day over 231 days.
  • v3.9’s Dubai reading failed its own forward test. It published the sour contango as a market signal and set the test at the 1 November roll: a roll-driven step should partly unwind there. It unwound — +2.44 (29 Oct) became −0.96, +0.13, −1.26. The crossing was the roll; §05 records the retraction.
  • The heating oil award landed short, and Washington changed hands. DOE awarded 300,000 barrels of the 315,000 authorised, from Boston and New Haven only. Democrats took both chambers; Vance blamed the war for the defeat and Schumer said Democrats would end it.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. This week the mechanism was demonstrated and the instrument bypassed in the same four sessions: the price moved a fifth on words, and none of the words were DOE’s.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

The cleanest natural experiment the paper has had. In four sessions the front month lost 17.8% — more than the entire 172 mb release was ever argued to be worth — on a circulating draft and an election result, then regained 5.1% in one session on a presidential threat. The reserve’s latest print, for the week to 30 October, is 0.399 mb; none yet covers the collapse week. That gap is itself the finding — the stock series cannot move at the speed the price just did. Realised lift is 0.58 mb/day over 231 days against an advertised 1.43. Announcements move this market by an order of magnitude more than barrels, which is why a policy built on announcements was rational — and why it confers no durable control.

→ Implication · suppression works, and it is cheap, but the lever is not owned — and it moves both ways. Whoever can move the war headline moves the price; DOE spent 172 mb to borrow that lever for eight months.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
weakening

This claim lost its best recent evidence to this paper’s own test. v3.9 read the Dubai crossing as a market signal and said a roll artefact would partly unwind at the 1 November roll. It unwound: −0.96, +0.13, −1.35. The sour prompt is back in backwardation and the sweet leg narrowed only because the curve collapsed beneath it — −4.12 to −2.40 on a $20 fall in flat price. Underneath, the measurement problem hardened: three further wire prints match COA exactly and a fourth matches the WTI CLA column, so both “front month” columns here are the wrong ones.

→ Implication · the spread cannot carry this claim until the archive is restated onto COA. Until then the claim rests on the stock series alone, which is weaker evidence and should be said so.

The programme’s exposure is the return leg, not the floor
restated

Restated this edition, as v3.9 recommended. Weeks-to-floor is not a live constraint in any scenario — 206 weeks at this draw, 309 at the three-week average — so the question moves to the other direction. DOE says 216 mb returns from early next year, including 44 mb of premium worth more than $4 billion. That premium is 25.6% of the 172 mb released, consistent with the 25% DOE cites elsewhere, so the claim is arithmetically coherent. The record is not: the return leg has booked zero barrels since the first quarter, and the price at which counterparties must buy them back fell a fifth and bounced inside one week.

→ Implication · a falling flat price makes returning barrels cheaper for the borrower and the exchange more likely to settle in kind — but Friday showed how fast that advantage is withdrawn. The first return prints are the test.

What this implies

A reserve that moved 0.4 mb in the week its programme was declared complete is not a supply instrument, and this week it was not a price instrument either: the market repriced by a fifth and back on politics while the reserve stood still. The awarded barrels will appear in this ledger as deliveries or not at all.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock is irrelevant, the policy clock has gone quiet, and the clock that moved the price this week is the electoral one — in both directions.

The US buffer clock is now irrelevant. DOESSPR printed 282.18 mb for 30 October, down 0.399 mb; no new print since. The ~200 mb floor sits 82.2 mb below stock: 206 weeks at that draw, 309 at the three-week average, both past the point of meaning. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb sits 29.8 mb below stock and binds only the Secretary’s limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, supported from a different direction by the GAO’s June finding of drawdown capability at 61% of design. Delivering the awarded 40 mb would take the reserve to 242.2 mb, 10.2 mb below the EPCA reference and 42.2 above the house floor.

The policy clock has gone quiet, and its last act came up short. One publication in the window. On 3 November, displayed as the 4th, DOE announced awards from the Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve: 300,000 barrels of ULSD against the 315,000 authorised on 21 October, from Boston and New Haven — South Portland, named in the authorisation, is absent — transfers “scheduled to be completed by November 6, 2026”, a week inside the RFP’s own deadline, the same volume returned with a premium. Nothing else was published: no awardee names on the 40 mb exchange, no schedule, no return dates. The drawer that emptied in eight days at the end of October has produced one 300,000-barrel item since.

The sour signal was taken back by the calendar. v3.9 published the Dubai crossing as market rather than roll and named the test: the 1 November roll would partly unwind a roll-driven step. The basis went +2.44 (29 Oct) to −0.96, +0.13, −1.35, −1.26 — a swing of 3.8 across the roll against a crossing worth 3.4. The reading is withdrawn; §05 records it. What survives is the physical story, which never depended on the spread: the East–West pipeline has run at its full 7 mb/day capacity since late March against ~2 before the war, and Aramco wants 10. This paper no longer claims to see that in the basis.

The electoral clock is the one that moved the price. On 3 November Democrats took both chambers. Vance named the war as the reason — “the real reason we lost is obvious to almost everyone” — and Schumer said Democrats would end it. The move began a day earlier: Iranian negotiators left Doha on 1 November and a draft began circulating; Qatari mediators flew to Tehran that night. Iran’s terms are unchanged but compressed — Baghaei said the seven-step plan “is now condensed, making it possible to implement it within two days” — and still require the blockade lifted first. Against that, US forces struck Qeshm Island on 2 November, called limited and defensive by two anonymous officials, answering an attack on a US aircraft; ABC reports similar strikes in recent weeks were deliberately not publicised.

And then the trade was doubted. Iran’s Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei said on the 3rd that “America’s response to Iran’s rights and demands was not convincing”, that Washington must “fully and explicitly address Iran’s seven conditions”, and that “only in this case will the Strait of Hormuz be reopened”. Qatar is facilitating “the real-time exchange of proposals”, but no new round is dated. Meanwhile the strait got worse: UKMTO logged at least five vessel incidents from Sunday, JMIC four attacks in 96 hours off Musandam, and the IRGC claimed four tankers mined on the 4th and two more plus a tug alight on the 5th — unverified, the same pattern v3.9 flagged. On the 5th, after what two officials called his first Iran meeting since the vote, Trump said he would “rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I’ll have to”, adding “you’ll know soon enough” — no timetable, no strikes ordered. Eurasia Group cut the odds of a deal from 20% to 10%, and of renewed US strikes only from 65% to 60%. Brent gapped higher on Friday and settled +5.12%. The peace trade of 2–4 November was priced ahead of its own preconditions.

The following report drew across crude, gasoline and distillate with demand at a one-year high, which is the other reason Friday’s rebound had a floor under it.

What this implies

Three questions carry the next move: whether the awarded 40 mb appears in the stock series or stays on paper, whether a draft survives Iran’s blockade precondition, and what a Congress elected to end the war does to a programme that needs 216 mb back.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

One test this paper set itself a week ago was answered against the paper, and one correction this paper attempted to make to itself was wrong. Both are recorded first.

The forward test v3.9 set on the Dubai basis was answered on 2 November, and the answer was no. The crossing did not survive the roll; it was a roll artefact, published as a market signal with a flag that Oman could not corroborate it. The flag was correct and the publication was wrong: the paper should have withheld the point rather than published it with a caveat. The dated test on the heating oil bids was also answered — 300,000 barrels of 315,000, two sites of three.

The second entry is a correction this paper tried to make and had to withdraw. On wire reports that Brent rose about 3.6% on “Thursday”, against a 5 November row showing a $1.32 range and a 0.5% fall, that row was provisionally read as an incomplete capture and the weekly point moved back to the 4th. The 6 November pull settles it the other way: 5 November is carried unchanged, and the only five differences in the whole shared history are blank cells filling in. The session was real, and quiet. The rally those outlets describe is the session this pull dates 6 November — COA 98.87 open, 100.83 high, 100.12 settle — which brackets every figure they print. Wire copy filed from Asia attributes a session to the previous calendar day; the data was right and the check was wrong.

The 17 mb question, closed by arithmetic. DOE has never said whether the 17 mb offered on 16 October sits inside the 40 mb award. Its own totals answer it: five earlier solicitations awarded “more than 133 million barrels”, and 133 + 40 leaves no room for a separate tranche inside a 172 mb commitment declared complete. An inference, not a disclosure.

Return risk — now the main exposure. DOE says 216 mb comes back from early next year including 44 mb of premium; nothing has returned since the first quarter. The flat price fell 17.8% and bounced 5.1% inside a week. A borrower who took barrels at $110 and can replace them at $92 has every incentive to settle and none to hurry — and Friday showed how fast that window closes.

Measurement risk — now settled, and not in the paper’s favour. Three further wire prints match COA exactly this week, taking it to seven, and a new kind arrived with them: the reported WTI settle of $88.12 on 4 November is this pull’s CLA column to the cent. Both “A” columns are the quoted front months; both “1” columns are not. Fig. 3 takes the first step of the remedy, with no historical value altered; §05 lists the matches.

What this implies

The week’s two methodological findings point opposite ways: a signal published seven days ago did not survive seven days, and a correction attempted yesterday did not survive the next pull. Published too early, withdrawn too early. Behind them the mechanism this paper has described all year was demonstrated at full scale in both directions — by two sentences, not by a reserve.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. This week neither was required. The price fell a fifth because people found out about it, and rose a twentieth three days later because the President said something else — which is what a signalling instrument is for, and which is also why the one holding 282 million barrels was not the one holding the lever.
