What changed — week to 30 October
  • The awards landed, and the exchange became a profit. On 29 October DOE announced awards of more than 40 mb from Big Hill and Bryan Mound, “including volumes offered through previous solicitations”. Under Secretary Haustveit: they “complete our commitment to release 172 million barrels” and have “secured the return of more than 216 million barrels… including 44 million additional premium barrels… with a value of more than $4 billion”. No companies, no schedule, and a return leg that has booked zero since the first quarter.
  • The barrels, meanwhile, all but stopped. SPR 282.58 mb on 23 October, down 0.100 mb — 0.014 mb/day, the smallest non-zero draw of the programme. Delivering the awarded 40 mb would take the reserve to 242.6 mb, 9.8 mb below the 252.4 mb EPCA reference.
  • The sour market crossed into surplus. Dubai’s basis printed +$2.44 on 29 October, a fifth straight positive session — the first since 28 July, after 57 of backwardation. Brent stayed negative at −$4.08. The bypass is not new: the East–West pipeline has run at its 7 mb/day capacity since March, against ~2 pre-war.
  • The price ran anyway. The front month gained 5.2% in a single session on 23 October, when Brent settled at $116.72, its highest since late April, and ended the week at $111.63 on the CO1 column, its highest since 18 May. Aramco ordered its Dhahran headquarters evacuated.
  • Washington called it a fake. On 22 October Trump said Iran was “making up fake hits, mines, and everything else” to drive up the oil price before the midterms. That is this paper’s thesis pointed the other way.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. This week the signal was spent completely — a declaration that the release is finished and will come back larger — while the barrels moved 0.10 mb. Awarding is not delivering, and returning is not awarding.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

The week supplied the clearest example yet of the gap this claim is about. In a session when the front month rose 5.2%, the reserve delivered 0.10 mb; six days later DOE announced awards of more than 40 mb and declared the release complete. Realised lift to date is 0.59 mb/day over 224 days against an advertised 1.43. The announcement arrived on schedule for the political calendar; the barrels are a quarter of what was advertised.

→ Implication · the award settles the take-up question carried since June — the barrels were wanted. It does not settle delivery, the only part the stock series sees.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
contested

The sour leg crossed zero. Dubai printed +$2.44 on 29 October, a fifth straight positive session, while Brent held at −$4.08 — the two benchmarks now disagree on sign, not merely degree. Nothing the reserve did caused it: the SPR drew a tenth of a million barrels while the Saudi bypass ran full at 7 mb/day, as it has since March. The measurement problem also hardened: a fourth wire print this week matches the COA column exactly, so the series this claim rests on is a calendar spread, not a prompt–physical premium.

→ Implication · the spread is now measuring a sour market in surplus and a sweet one still tight. Neither reading is about the reserve, and the claim cannot be adjudicated until the series is restated.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
weakening

The question has inverted. Measured in weeks the floor is unreachable — 826 at this week’s draw, 619 at the three-week average. But the award gives it a hard number for the first time: delivering the awarded 40 mb takes the reserve to 242.6 mb, which is 9.8 mb below the 252.4 mb EPCA statutory reference and 42.6 above the house floor. And DOE now says 216 mb returns from early next year.

→ Implication · restate this claim for v3.10 as a question about the return, not the floor. The reserve’s 2026 outcome now turns on whether 44 mb of premium barrels actually arrive.

What this implies

A reserve that moves a tenth of a million barrels in a week is not a supply instrument, and the market it was pointed at has resolved its tightness without it: the sour prompt is in surplus on Saudi pipeline volume. The awards announced on the 29th will appear in this ledger as deliveries or not at all — and on the evidence of 2026, the lag between an award and a barrel is the programme’s defining feature.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock has stopped mattering, the policy clock emptied its drawer in eight days, and every other clock now runs to 3 November.

The US buffer clock has stopped mattering. DOESSPR printed 282.58 mb for 23 October, down 0.100 mb. The ~200 mb floor sits 82.6 mb below current stock: 826 weeks at this week’s draw, 619 at the three-week average. Both are past the point of meaning. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb sits ~30 mb below current stock and binds only the Secretary’s limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here; DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, supported from a different direction by the GAO’s June finding of drawdown capability at 61% of design.

The policy clock emptied its drawer. Four actions in eight days. On 21 October DOE authorised up to 315,000 barrels of ultra-low sulfur distillate from the Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve, from South Portland, Boston and New Haven, deliveries complete by 13 November and bids closed on the 27th; Secretary Wright called it “a temporary measure to solve a temporary supply disruption”. On the 22nd it announced up to $150 million of Defense Production Act Title III funding to expand refining capacity, with recipients not yet selected. On the 23rd it authorised an emergency exchange of up to 400,000 barrels from West Hackberry to the Phillips 66 Lake Charles refinery, for immediate delivery and return with premium. And on the 29th came the awards: more than 40 mb from Big Hill and Bryan Mound, “including volumes offered through previous solicitations”, which DOE says completes the 172 mb release. It does not name the awardees, give a delivery schedule, or say whether the 17 mb offered on the 16th is inside that number. The heating oil award is still unpublished.

The sour market solved itself. Dubai’s prompt discount has become a prompt premium: +$2.44 on 29 October against −$0.99 a week earlier, the first positive print since 28 July and now five sessions old. The mechanism is sustained, not new: the East–West pipeline carried about 2 mb/day before the war, reached its full 7 mb/day capacity by late March and has been held there, routing Saudi barrels to the Red Sea instead of the strait. What is new is the intent — Amin Nasser said this week Aramco is working to lift capacity to 10 mb/day, two to three years on the energy minister’s estimate. A bypass at capacity for seven months is what a prompt sour surplus looks like. Brent did not follow — it held at −$4.08. The two benchmarks disagreeing on sign is the clearest signal in this edition, and no reserve barrel is in it.

Every other clock runs to 3 November. Qatari mediators delivered a draft to Tehran on 25 October; Araghchi said on the 28th that Iran was working with mediators on a seven-day plan to end the war and reopen the strait, possibly “in even less than a week”, with its conditions unchanged — a region-wide ceasefire, assets unfrozen, oil sanctions lifted, the naval blockade ended. Iranian officials briefed Reuters in contradictory terms, one calling Qatar’s paper “unacceptable ideas and not an offer”. A four-way meeting in Tehran on the 26th — Iran’s Rezaei with Pakistan’s Munir, Saudi Arabia’s Prince Khalid bin Salman and Turkey’s Kalin — ended only in a commitment to keep talking, though Reuters reports Munir carried a proposal to end hostilities that had Trump’s approval, with four states as guarantors. The Wall Street Journal reports Washington has told Gulf states to expect a possible “short, intense military campaign” after the vote, with no decision taken. And on 22 October Trump accused Iran of “making up fake hits, mines, and everything else” to raise the oil price before the election, arguing more crude was moving through the strait than before the war — which Kpler contradicts, putting September transits near 9 mb/day, about 44% of February. A week later, on the 29th, Iranian state television carried a Revolutionary Guard claim that three tankers had struck mines within 24 hours on an unauthorised route. AP could not confirm it and the vessels were not identified; UKMTO saw one ship on fire without establishing whether it was among the three, and Lloyd’s List reported tankers still transiting under US escort. The claim and the accusation now sit directly on top of one another, and this paper can verify neither.

Products are where the shortfall still is. EIA put commercial crude at 410.6 mb for the week to 23 October, down 1.9 mb; distillate fell 1.5 mb to 102.8, giving back the prior week’s build; gasoline rose 3.9 mb to 209.2, with refineries at 92.8%. Three Russian diesel tankers are crossing the Atlantic, the first bound for New York; Novak said on the 24th that Russian companies could supply 500,000 tonnes in October, where Trump’s schedule had said 300,000. On the Bloomberg series US average gasoline was $5.11 on 28 October, down from the $5.35 peak on 24 September.

What this implies

Three questions carry the next move: whether the awarded barrels appear in the weekly stock series or sit on paper, whether the Russian schedule survives contact with loadings data, and what happens to the deferred military branch after 3 November.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

One claim is answered on its own metric and should be retired. The week's real finding is a market that cleared without the instrument.

The dated test this paper has carried since the start of October was answered on 29 October, twenty-three days after the 40 mb bids closed: the barrels were awarded, in full and then some. This edition corrects a draft that had read the silence as absent demand — the silence was DOE’s publication lag, not the market’s. Two tests remain open: whether the 17 mb offered on the 16th sits inside the 40 mb award, which DOE does not say, and whether the heating oil bids produced anything at all. A third now opens: the 216 mb return, due to begin early next year against a return leg that has booked nothing since March.

Soft branch recovering

  • Present a Qatari draft in Tehran (25 Oct); Araghchi offers “less than a week”
  • Present Dubai basis +$2.44, five sessions of sour surplus
  • Absent nothing signed; Iran’s conditions unchanged

Hard branch winning

  • Scheduled Gulf states told to expect a campaign after 3 Nov
  • Present Aramco HQ evacuated; front month +5.2% in a session
  • Thin SPR 0.10 mb delivered against >40 mb awarded

Exhaustion risk — the question moved to the return. At 619–826 weeks the floor is not a constraint in any scenario this conflict can produce, and take-up is now answered: June’s round placed about half a million barrels of 40 mb, but the October award placed more than 40 mb outright. What is unresolved is the other direction. DOE says 216 mb comes back from early next year, including 44 mb of premium; the return leg has recorded nothing since the first quarter. v3.10 should track returns, not weeks-to-floor.

Measurement risk — still the largest. A fourth independent print matched the COA column this week: the 23 October settle of $116.72 for Brent’s December contract, its highest since late April. CO1 matched none of the four. On that evidence every “Brent front month” figure in v3.0–v3.9 names the wrong contract and the basis series is a calendar spread. The conclusions do not turn on the level, but the second claim turns on the spread, and the archive needs restating.

Escalation risk — dated. A campaign Gulf states have been warned to prepare for, after an election five days away, against a diplomatic track that has produced its first text. Both branches are live in the same fortnight, and the flat price rose through a week of diplomatic progress — which says the market is pricing the 4 November branch, not the 28 October one.

What this implies

The reserve is no longer a bystander — it has just committed more than 40 mb and claimed a $4 billion return. Whether that is a supply event or an accounting one becomes visible in the weekly stock series within a month, which is the one test this paper can run unaided.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. This week it was promised in both directions at once: 172 mb declared spent, 216 mb declared returning. Conservation of matter sets the clock; politics sets the hour; and a barrel awarded is still, until it moves, a sentence.

Conventions. Prices, spreads, SPR levels and gasoline come only from the Tecity Bloomberg pull; news and context are web-sourced and separately verified. Basis = front minus spot as the pull labels those columns, so negative is backwardation. Dubai = DBL1−DBLA. SPR = DOESSPR. Operational floor = the ~200 mb Tecity estimate (analyst Zilin); cushion = level minus 200. The weekly point is the most recent session in the pull — 30 October — while Fig. 2 keeps the both-legs rule and is dated 29 October, the last session on which Dubai printed. The Dubai
sign flip
Read as market, not roll — but with a caveat that cannot be closed. The basis went −0.99 (21 Oct) to +4.06 (23 Oct), then +2.45, +0.56, +2.45, +2.44 — five sessions above zero. Three things argue market: it fell mid-month, not at the month turn where every prior roll step sits; both legs rose sharply (DBL +8.11, DBLA +3.06) where a roll moves one and leaves the other; and it then decayed rather than holding a shifted level. Against that, Oman cannot corroborate it — Oman’s basis equals Brent’s to the cent on 109 of 205 sessions, including 19–26 October, so Gate 7 fails and the independent check this print most needs is unavailable. The point is published and flagged; a reader who prefers to withhold it should treat the Dubai line as ending on 21 October. The 1 November roll falls after this edition and will test it: a roll-driven step should partly unwind there, a market one should not.
