What changed — week to 16 October
  • The reserve did not draw. SPR 282.98 mb on 9 October, unchanged from 2 October — the first flat weekly print since March. On 16 October DOE offered up to 17 mb, which Reuters reports is a portion of the 40 mb whose bids closed on 6 October; Energy Aspects infers that about 23 mb was awarded. DOE has published no award. A separate 4 mb storm loan went to ExxonMobil and BP on 10 October.
  • The price ran. The front month closed at $106.82 on 16 October, +8.7% since 8 October and the highest close since 19 May; it rose 3.5% on the 16th alone, as a missile aimed at Yanbu was intercepted and attacks on shipping continued.
  • The premium split by crude. Brent’s basis held between −$2.66 and −$3.47 all week and ended at −$3.04. Dubai’s collapsed to −$1.05, the narrowest since 28 July, from −$8.08 at the first October print.
  • Ships sank and offices burned. Singapore’s maritime authority said the GFS Pride was hit on 13 October, caught fire and sank, all 21 crew evacuated. A Houthi missile hit the top floors of Aramco’s Dhahran headquarters on 13 October, without casualties.
  • Policy moved on diesel, in both directions. The IEA’s Governing Board agreed on 15 October to speed up the remaining ~100 mb of the March action, including over 20 mb of diesel; its October report keeps gasoil tight well into 2027. Then on 16–17 October Trump waived the Russia sanctions and tariffs days from taking effect, against Russian diesel for world markets — and Brent gave back about 1% on the 19th, the first policy move of this conflict the price fell on.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. This week the barrels stopped — the reserve printed no draw — and the signal kept going: a 17 mb re-offer and an accelerated IEA release, announced while the flat price rose 8.7%.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

The week separated the barrels from the announcements. The SPR delivered nothing in the week to 9 October. DOE’s response to rising prices was to re-offer 17 mb of the 40 mb nobody fully took; the IEA accelerated the rest of the March action with over 20 mb of diesel first. Realised lift to date is 0.63 mb/day over 210 days against an advertised 1.43, with no return barrels since the first quarter.

→ Implication · the instrument now works mostly as announcement. If re-offers keep coming without published awards, the signal is most of what is left of it.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
contested

The flat price rose 8.7% to a five-month high with no SPR draw at all, yet the prompt premium did not blow out. Brent’s basis stayed in a −$2.66 to −$3.47 band — in line with early October and far narrower than the −$7.45 of 28 September — and Dubai’s narrowed to −$1.05. With nothing coming out of the reserve, the spread cannot be measuring the reserve. It moves with Gulf flows, which Kpler had near pre-war levels at the start of the month, while the war is priced in the outright.

→ Implication · the spread has become a flow gauge, not a suppression gauge. A widening from here would signal routing breaking down, not the reserve running dry.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
contested

A flat print is what an exhausted book looks like, and the conflict escalated in the same week. The room on paper is also smaller than offered: if Energy Aspects is right that only ~23 mb of the 40 mb was awarded, delivery takes the reserve to about 260.0 mb — 60.0 mb above the house floor and still 7.6 mb above the 252.4 mb EPCA statutory reference. Only if the re-offered 17 mb is also taken does the reserve reach ~243.0 mb and cross it.

→ Implication · the 17 mb re-offer decides whether the statutory line is crossed. A second thin take-up would mean the market itself has stopped wanting the room.

What this implies

The reserve’s crude barrels and the crude price have now moved apart: no draw, and an 8.7% rise. What policy produced this week was mostly paper — a 17 mb re-offer and an accelerated IEA timetable — plus a 4 mb storm loan; the part of the market that responded to physical supply was the sour crude premium, which narrowed on Gulf flows, not on US barrels. Watch the award notices and the diesel tranche.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock paused on a partial take-up, the policy clock is accelerating on diesel, and the war clock reached Aramco’s headquarters.

The US buffer clock paused. DOESSPR printed 282.98 mb for 9 October, unchanged on the week; EIA’s release slipped to 15 October for Columbus Day. DOE has published no award on the up-to-40 mb exchange whose bids closed on 6 October, but on 16 October it re-offered up to 17 mb of it; Energy Aspects reads that as ~23 mb awarded. Separately, on 10 October DOE lent up to 4 mb to ExxonMobil and BP for weather disruption in the Gulf, partly by accelerating previously awarded deliveries — which may show in the next print. The ~200 mb floor sits 83.0 mb below current stock: 106 weeks at the last non-zero draw (−0.78 mb) and 159 at the three-week average including the flat week. Delivering ~23 mb would leave ~260.0 mb; the full 40 would leave ~243.0 mb, the “roughly 243 million” Fortune reported on 12 October.

Which floor — and can it be reached? The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here; it sits ~31 mb below current stock. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer. The GAO’s June report supports that from a different direction: a growing maintenance backlog, no long-term review since 2016, ageing equipment that will increasingly limit fill and drawdown, and drawdown capability at 61% of design; DOE agreed to reassess its drawdown and refill criteria.

The policy clock accelerated, diesel first. Birol wrote to members on 11 October urging faster delivery of the March pledges and asking all members to weigh supplemental diesel releases. On 15 October the Governing Board, with ~330 mb released so far, agreed to speed up the remaining ~100 mb, including over 20 mb of diesel as soon as possible; it announced no new volume beyond the March action. The two figures exceed the original 400 mb because several members have released more than they pledged, and the Board expects the total to run past it. The IEA’s October report puts September gasoil exports ~700 kb/day below a year earlier, keeps the market tight well into 2027, and judges Russia’s partial easing of its diesel export ban unlikely to change the balance — an assessment written before 16–17 October, when Trump waived the Russia sanctions and tariffs then days from taking effect, including penalties on buying Russian oil and gas, against Russian diesel for world markets. That puts US-brokered Russian barrels and the IEA’s 20 mb tranche into the same tight market, and it is the first policy move of this conflict the price fell on.

The war clock reached the producers. Singapore’s MPA said the GFS Pride was struck on 13 October, caught fire and sank, its 21 crew evacuated; the GFS Giselle, hit the next day, reached anchorage under its own power, with no injuries on either ship. The IRGC said a container ship struck a mine and sank; trade press links that claim to the GFS Pride, though it is not established. Two senior Iranian officials told Reuters that Tehran has shifted tactics to keep prices high before the US vote. A Houthi missile hit the top floors of Aramco’s Dhahran headquarters on the night of the 13th, and on the 16th the coalition said it intercepted a missile aimed at Yanbu, the Red Sea port Saudi Arabia uses to bypass the strait. Egypt pledged support for Riyadh on the 13th; on the 17th Sisi asked parliament to authorise deploying troops.

The diplomatic clock is waiting on Tehran. Iran’s foreign ministry said on 12 October it is still reviewing the US response and will reply through mediators, among them Qatar and Pakistan; a day earlier Mohsen Rezaei of the Supreme National Security Council called the response inadequate against the seven conditions. On 18 October foreign minister Araqchi said Tehran’s standing offer — made in mid-September, to reopen the strait within six to seven days if its conditions are met — is still on the table, and that Washington has sent comments Iran is reviewing. The conditions are unchanged: lifting the port blockade and the sanctions on Iranian oil exports, and an end to hostilities on all regional fronts. Nothing is signed.

Products are the tight leg and the policy target. EIA put distillate stocks at 102.6 mb in the week to 9 October, down 2.5 mb and about 12% below the five-year average, while commercial crude fell 8.6 mb. On the Bloomberg series US average gasoline was $5.19 on 15 October, against a $5.35 peak on 24 September. Diesel is where the IEA’s 20 mb goes first; crude is where the price moved.

What this implies

Three things carry the next move: the take-up on the 17 mb re-offer, which decides whether the statutory reference is crossed; the pace of the IEA’s 20 mb diesel tranche, now against US-brokered Russian barrels as well as distillate stocks; and whether attacks reach Red Sea loading at Yanbu, which would hit the bypass itself. The next SPR print, for 16 October, will show whether the flat week was a pause, and how much the storm loan moved.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

No claim changed status. The week tested the first two hardest: a price spike with no barrels behind the response.

The dated test this paper set — the 6 October bid count — has still not been published, but the 16 October re-offer answers it indirectly: the 40 mb was not fully taken, and Energy Aspects puts the award near 23 mb. That is an inference, and this paper marks it as one. What the week settled is that the crude price and the reserve have separated: a flat print and an 8.7% rise in the same edition. That supports the first claim, and leaves the second contested, because the spread did not widen either.

Soft branch recovering

  • Present Dubai basis −$1.05 (16 Oct), narrowest since 28 July
  • Absent no signed text; Iran says the US response misses its conditions
  • Absent transit counts thin; the strait is not open

Hard branch winning

  • Present GFS Pride sunk; Aramco HQ hit; Yanbu targeted; tanker hit off Jubail
  • Present front month $106.82, highest since 19 May
  • Thin SPR flat; 17 mb of the 40 re-offered (~23 mb taken, inferred)

Exhaustion risk — the book has stopped. The barrels and the signal are still spent in the same act; this week only the signal was spent. If ~23 mb was awarded, the reserve stays above the statutory reference on delivery; if the 17 mb re-offer clears too, it crosses it. A second thin round — June’s took about half a million barrels of 40 mb offered — would mean the reserve’s practical contribution is close to its end at ~132 mb plus whatever the September round delivers.

Positioning risk — the outright carries the war. The flat price absorbed the week’s attacks while the sour prompt premium kept closing. Exposure to the conflict sits in the outright, diesel and freight, not in crude curve structure.

Escalation risk — the bypass is now in range. UKMTO reported one or two tankers hit off Jubail on 16 October, and the IRGC claimed two tankers struck mines on the 17th, without independent confirmation. A missile aimed at Yanbu puts the Red Sea leg of the bypass — 17% of Gulf crude in September, by Kpler’s split — inside the war. No reserve can replace a bypass. Discount definitive claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The fat tail has moved from the strait to the routes around it and to products. A reserve holding crude, and now not drawing it, hedges neither.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.

Conventions, the caveats a reader must carry, and this week's verification only. Earlier weeks' ledgers stay in the version files they were published in.

Conventions. Prices, spreads, SPR levels and gasoline come only from the Tecity Bloomberg pull; news and context are web-sourced and separately verified. Brent front-month = CO1; basis = front minus spot, so positive is contango and negative backwardation. Dubai = DBL1−DBLA. SPR = DOESSPR. Operational floor = the ~200 mb Tecity estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb conventionally cited; cushion = level minus 200. The weekly point is the most recent session in the pull — 16 October, when both legs printed, so Fig. 2 is on the same date. This edition is measured against v3.6 (8 October), the last published version; a 15 October draft of v3.7 is superseded. Revision of 19 October: this file corrects the 16 October build of v3.7 after a full re-audit against the workbook. Three Dubai superlatives move from August to 28 July, the August swap window is restated as 3–28 August, the 31 August duplicate pair is disclosed, and two news items are added. No plotted point and no derived figure changes.

Vendor label and wire disagree; if COA is the front-month settle, CO1 is a later contract and the basis is a calendar spread — still negative in backwardation, but a different measure.
