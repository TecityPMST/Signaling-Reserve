What changed — week to 24 September
  • The bypass reopened. Aramco restarted Petroline on 22 September at a reduced rate, with a first Yanbu cargo loading for China and full capacity weeks away. Saudi barrels had already found the other way out: flows through Hormuz itself ran at ~2.9 mb/day against ~700 kb/d in August.
  • Tehran named a price for the strait. Iran conveyed conditions to Washington through Qatar and Pakistan — end the war on all fronts, release frozen funds, lift the naval blockade — and a senior official said the strait could reopen within seven days if pressure eased. Mediators shuttled between the delegations at the UN on 22–23 September. No text; Trump suggested any deal waits until after the midterms.
  • Brent fell five sessions, then turned. The front month ran $100.76 (16 Sep) to $95.41 (22 Sep) — which the wires, quoting spot, called the first sub-$100 close since 9 September — then $98.12 on the 23rd as Iran vowed it would not surrender and $97.40 on the 24th, −3.3% on the week. The weekly point now follows the latest session in the pull; Fig. 2 keeps the both-legs rule (§05).
  • The two bases split for the first time in five weeks. Brent’s prompt premium eased to −$4.96 from −$5.07; Dubai’s deepened to a record −$17.89 on 21 September before ending the week at −$15.88. The relief reached the Atlantic basin. East of Suez it did not.
  • The reserve drew 0.4 mb; the policy argument moved to diesel. SPR 284.55 mb (18 Sep), −0.41 on the week, ~2.6 mb of contracted barrels left. Diesel set another record near $6.51–6.52. Politico reported a 90-day diesel export ban in preparation; the White House called that inaccurate, the Treasury Secretary said it was being examined, the Energy Secretary said no flat ban was under consideration.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. This week delivered the clean test the argument had been waiting for. The first sustained fall in the price of the entire episode arrived — five straight falls, −5.3% at the 22 September trough and −3.3% on the week — and it came from a repaired pipeline and a conditional offer from Tehran. The reserve’s contribution was four hundred thousand barrels, with ~2.6 mb of contracted volume left. Relief came from routes and diplomacy, which is where it was always going to come from.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

Realised lift now averages 0.69 mb/day over 189 days against an advertised 1.43, peaking at ~1.31 in May, with no return barrels since February. The programme is finished in practice: −0.41 mb on the week to 284.55 mb, against −6.1 mb in a single week in early August, leaving roughly 2.6 mb of contracted barrels. The week that the price finally fell is the week the reserve was least involved in it. No DOE statement calls the programme finished — the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on about half a million barrels, and the awarded book has simply run out — but Energy Secretary Wright said on 14 September that refilling “will start in the next few months,” and this week the administration was arguing about diesel export controls, not crude releases.

→ Implication · the suppression ended as arithmetic before it ended as policy, and the instrument has already been replaced. When the state wants to move fuel prices now it reaches for trade policy, because the barrels are gone.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
contested

The two bases diverged for the first time in five weeks. Brent’s premium eased to −$4.96 from −$5.07, touching −$3.84 on 22 September and printing −$4.76 on the 24th; Dubai’s deepened to a record −$17.89 on the 21st before ending at −$15.88. The easing traces to physical routing rather than to policy: Petroline restarted on 22 September, and Saudi crude was already moving through the strait at ~2.9 mb/day against ~700 kb/d in August. The reserve is not in this picture at any point.

→ Implication · the basis now reads route repair, not reserve policy. A narrowing Brent basis alongside a record Dubai basis is a freight-and-routing signal, and it says the physical relief stops at the strait.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
strengthened

The conflict produced its first credible reopening offer this week — seven days, conditional on lifting the blockade — and it arrived with the programme already spent: ~2.6 mb contracted, 0.41 mb drawn, no replacement tender. Whatever the diplomacy delivers now, it arrives after the instrument was exhausted rather than because of it. Weeks-to-floor reads 68–124 — long because the draw stopped, not because the buffer grew.

→ Implication · read the runway as exhaustion, not recovery. If the strait reopens on Tehran’s terms, the US will have spent 131 mb to bridge to a settlement it did not shape.

What this implies

The price fell this week for the first time in five, and the reserve had nothing to do with it. What moved the market was a weld at Yanbu, a shuttle of tankers through the strait the bypass existed to avoid, and a conditional sentence from Tehran. Headline exposure should still be sized as though the buffer were absent, because as a price-setting instrument it is — but the direction of surprise has changed: the same routing that took the top off the price can be taken away again by a single drone, and the Dubai basis says the east-of-Suez market does not believe the relief. Watch the Petroline ramp rate and the Hormuz transit count, not the reserve.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

The buffer clock has stopped, the route clock turned favourable, and a diplomatic clock started for the first time since the memorandum lapsed.

The US buffer clock is now a backstop, not a countdown. ~2.6 mb of contracted deliveries remain of the ~133.5 mb awarded, with no replacement since the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on about half a million barrels. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 84.6 mb below current stock — at the last three weekly prints (−1.24, −0.40, −0.41 mb) that is 68–124 weeks, against 71–104 a week ago on the same method. The runway lengthened because the programme stopped, not because the buffer grew. The 4 September print has now arrived in the Bloomberg feed at 285.36 mb, closing the gap flagged last week, so the two-week row splits cleanly into −1.24 and −0.40. The week’s point is beyond argument: the price fell 3.3% on the week, and 5.3% at the 22 September trough, while the reserve released four hundred thousand barrels.

Which floor? Three numbers circulate. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here — it sits ~32 mb below current stock. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability at 61% of design before ~126 mb came out. The dispute is public: Hochstein said on 15 August that going below 300 damages the caverns; DOE rebutted that “the caverns are always full — all that changes is the ratio of oil and water.” The reserve is now below 285.

The routes reopened — partially, and on a repair timetable. Aramco restarted the East–West (Petroline) line on 22 September at a reduced rate after eleven days down, with a first Yanbu cargo loading for China and full capacity put at weeks rather than days. The more telling number is the workaround that preceded it: Saudi crude moved through Hormuz itself at ~2.9 mb/day against ~700 kb/d in August, with roughly 14 mb loaded onto tankers. The kingdom answered a pipeline outage by accepting chokepoint risk, and the market took $5 off the front month for it. That trade is reversible in an afternoon, which is why the Dubai basis has not followed the Brent basis down.

A diplomatic clock started. Iran conveyed conditions to Washington through Qatar and Pakistan — an end to the war on all fronts, the release of frozen funds, and an end to the naval blockade — and a senior official put reopening at seven days from the moment pressure eases. Mediators shuttled between the delegations at the UN General Assembly on 22–23 September, the first substantive contact since the June memorandum lapsed. Nothing is signed: Pezeshkian’s speech on the 23rd was defiant, Trump called for Iran’s economic isolation and suggested any deal would come after the November midterms, and the price promptly took back half its fall. Treat the seven-day claim as a negotiating position with a price attached, not a timetable.

The Iranian clock — rerouting, but not replacing. Kharg Island reactivated on 12 August after a 25-day halt and Caspian shipments from Russia have roughly tripled since the blockade began, but the volume has not followed: China’s imports of Iranian crude fell to 534 kb/d in August from 823 in July against a 1.4 mb/d 2025 average. The rial hit a record 2.02 m to the dollar on 24 August. Iran is rerouting around the blockade and the reroute is not replacing the barrels — which is the context for a seven-day offer conditioned on lifting that blockade.

Soft
live, unsigned
The arrangement signs and delivers free passage — no tolls, mines cleared, insurers treating the strait as open. For the first time since the memorandum lapsed there is a live thread: conditions conveyed through two mediators, a seven-day claim, and shuttle contact at the UN. There is still no text, and one to two vessels a day is not a reopening.
Optical
the live risk
Headline reopening while physical flow lags — escorted, tolled, or under Iranian management. The seven-day offer is exactly this risk in its newest form: a reopening that is permitted rather than restored, revocable at the granting party’s discretion. The market paid $5 for the possibility inside three sessions.
Hard
arrived, easing
Record prompt premiums with the outright above $100 — still the standing condition east of Suez, where Dubai set a record −$17.89 on 21 September. What changed is that the Atlantic basin got relief from a repaired pipeline while the SPR sat out the week with ~2.6 mb left: a dislocation landing into a market with neither cushion nor hedge, now partly patched by freight.

Products run on a separate clock underneath, and it is now the policy clock. On the Bloomberg series US average gasoline printed $5.34 on 22 September — but that series tracks roughly $0.90 above AAA’s regular-unleaded average, which was $4.44 on 17 September, up 16 cents on the week and still below its June 2022 record. The record is again diesel, near $6.51–6.52 on 21–23 September, with distillate stocks at 107.4 mb, about 12% below the five-year average. That gap is what turned fuel into a policy question this week: Politico reported a 90-day export ban in preparation, a White House spokesperson called the report inaccurate, Treasury Secretary Bessent said the administration was examining whether a full or partial ban would work, and Energy Secretary Wright said nobody was considering a flat ban and that one would raise gasoline and jet fuel prices. Refiners are lobbying against it. On 16 September the Federal Reserve raised rates 25bp to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since 2023 — a demand-side headwind pulling against the supply story. If a dislocation arrives from here it is likelier to present as a diesel crisis than a crude one, and the instrument reached for will be trade policy, because the reserve is gone.

What this implies

Two variables now carry the price: the Petroline ramp rate and the physical reopening — mines, escorts, insurers, ~430 stranded ships. Transit counts read both more honestly than headlines do, and at one to two a day they say the strait is still shut whatever was said in New York.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

The test this paper set finally half-fired — and the half that fired was not the half that matters.

The suppression thesis has played out in full: the draw ran to a 43-year low and through it, the corridor never opened, and with ~2.6 mb of contracted barrels the reserve is spent. The restated test — a basis narrowing while the outright falls, alongside transits in the dozens — fired on two legs this week for the first time. The outright fell 3.3% on the week — 5.3% at the 22 September trough — and Brent’s basis narrowed. The third leg failed outright: the count ran at one to two transits a day against a pre-crisis baseline of roughly 85–125, and Dubai’s basis set a record. Two legs out of three, with the physical leg missing, is what route repair looks like — not what reopening looks like.

Soft branch recovering

  • Present first easing of the Brent basis with the outright falling — on a pipeline restart, not a settlement
  • Present conditions conveyed via Qatar and Pakistan; shuttle contact at the UN, 22–23 Sep
  • Absent no signed text, no mine clearance, transits at one to two a day, Dubai basis at a record

Hard branch winning

  • Present record Dubai premium −$17.89 (21 Sep) — east of Suez saw no relief at all
  • Present diesel record near $6.51–6.52 with distillate ~12% below the five-year average
  • Exhausted SPR draw 0.4 mb/week with ~2.6 mb contracted left — the programme has stopped

Exhaustion risk — realised, and now visible in what replaced it. The paper has argued since v1 that the barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. The confirmation this week is not another record low; it is that when Washington wanted to act on fuel prices it debated banning diesel exports rather than releasing crude. An export restriction is the same instrument class as the exchange — a domestic price intervention with a finite, borrowed effect — reached for because the first one is empty. That it was floated, denied, examined and rejected inside thirty-six hours is itself the signal.

Positioning risk — the gap between the two basins is now the trade. Brent’s basis eased while Dubai’s set a record: the Atlantic priced a repaired pipeline, the Gulf priced a strait that is still shut. A hedge built around Hormuz reopening does not cover a pipeline outage, and one built around Brent structure no longer reflects east-of-Suez prompt scarcity. The Petroline ramp rate has become the single most price-sensitive variable in the complex, and it can be reversed by one drone.

Optical-deal risk — back, and sharper. The seven-day offer is the optical branch in its newest form. It is conditional on lifting a blockade, was relayed through mediators rather than signed, and moved the price before anything moved in the water. The correction mechanism is the transit count, which did not move at all.

Escalation risk. The tanker war continues, though the week’s only reported strike in the strait — an LPG tanker hit by projectile debris, two crew injured — rests on a single tracker relaying UKMTO and is carried here as unconfirmed. Iraq’s 30 September militia-disarmament deadline now sits directly on the price, days after drones launched from Iraqi territory took the bypass down. Discount definitive claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The distribution is no longer one-sided. A functioning bypass plus a live negotiation puts genuine downside into a market that had none for five weeks — but both are revocable, neither is signed, and there is still no instrument left to flatten a dislocation if they fail. Size the relief as borrowed, not banked.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.

Conventions, the caveats a reader must carry, and this week's verification only. Earlier weeks' ledgers stay in the version files they were published in.

Conventions. Prices, spreads, SPR levels and gasoline come only from the Tecity Bloomberg pull; news and context are web-sourced and separately verified. Brent front-month = CO1; basis = front minus spot, so positive is contango and negative backwardation. Dubai = DBL1−DBLA. SPR = DOESSPR. Operational floor = the ~200 mb Tecity estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb conventionally cited; cushion = level minus 200. Convention change, effective this edition: the weekly point is now the most recent session in the pull — 24 September — rather than the most recent session with a complete print in both legs, which had been the rule since v2.5. Fig. 2 alone retains the both-legs rule, because Dubai did not print on the 24th and a null would break the series most weeks; it is dated 23 September and the difference is stated in its caption. Headline basis figures follow Fig. 2. Stages 1–6 of the argument are in v2.11 §02.
