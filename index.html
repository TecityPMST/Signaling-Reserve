What changed — week to 27 August
  • A corridor, and an immediate denial. Iran announced a temporary seven-mile lane agreed with Oman on 25–26 August — inbound entirely through Iranian waters, 30–60 days, with an IRGC spokesman saying shares of the waters and revenues are agreed. Oman has announced nothing; coordinates, start date and revenue mechanics are undisclosed. Gharibabadi said on 25 August the strait does not reopen until Washington honours the June memorandum.
  • Crude round-tripped on diplomacy. Brent ran to $92.67 (21 Aug), then fell three sessions to $86.94 (26 Aug) and $86.30 on 27 August. “Operation Economic Outcast” landed ~60 designations on 24 August but removed no barrels and named no major Chinese bank; the corridor took the rest.
  • Iran blacklisted 45–46 tankers. Vessels of ADNOC, AD Ports, Bahri and Nakilat were named for breaching Iran's crossing rules, with threats of fines, detention and cargo confiscation extending to any third party doing ship-to-ship transfers with them — aimed squarely at the Gulf shuttle workaround.
  • The reserve drew again, more slowly. −3.7 mb to 289.7 mb (21 Aug), 40.6% of authorised capacity and the lowest since November 1982, at 0.53 mb/day. Contracted barrels down to ~7.8 mb; cushion 89.7 mb, 20–24 weeks.
  • The basins split. Brent's basis flattened to −$0.90 while Dubai widened to −$4.82; Oman moved with Dubai, so this is market, not the roll. Kpler counted 7, 2 and 5 transits on 21, 24 and 25 August against a ten-day average of 14–15; no full-week count was published, and the Lloyd's List and UKMTO series count different universes.

01Three claims, tracked weekly

Each claim carries its own evidence, its own status, and what it would imply if it holds. Status is set against the prior week, not against the paper's opening position.

The thesis, in one paragraph

In March 2026 the United States authorised a 172 mb SPR release, structured as an exchange rather than a sale, inside a coordinated 400 mb IEA action against the Hormuz closure. This paper has argued since v1 that it was never sized to replace lost supply — it cannot clear a 9–13 mb/day shortfall at any deliverable rate — and that its observable function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market with finite barrels and the forward curve with announcement effects, to buy time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. That act is complete: ~8 mb of contracted barrels remain, and what now moves the price is the negotiating table.

The release was sized to suppress price, not to replace supply
holding

Realised lift averaged 0.79 mb/day across the programme against an advertised 1.43, peaking at ~1.31 in the three record weeks of May. DOE's own movement ledger confirms 116.6 mb released March–August against no return barrels since February. The draw slowed to −3.7 mb, 289.4 mb in the week to 21 August — 0.53 mb/day, the slowest since 31 July, and consistent with a programme running out of contracted volume rather than one being throttled back by choice. Roughly 7.8 mb remain of the ~133.5 mb awarded.

→ Implication · the taper is exhaustion, not restraint. At this pace the contracted book empties within a fortnight.

Suppression is visible in the prompt–physical spread, and it is now failing
diverging

The basins split this week. Brent's basis flattened from −$1.83 to −$0.90 as the outright fell 6.2% from its 21 August peak — a narrowing basis with a falling front, which is the soft-branch signature the paper defined a week ago. Dubai went the other way, widening from −$2.33 to −$4.82, and Oman moved with it, so this is market rather than the Mini-Dubai roll (which steps at the month turn, an order of magnitude larger).

→ Implication · the Atlantic is pricing relief the Gulf is not. Positioning that treats Brent as the read on Hormuz is now reading the wrong basin.

The programme runs out of room before the conflict resolves
strengthened

The corridor is the test case. It is temporary, seven miles wide, entered through Iranian territorial waters, unannounced by Oman, and paired with a committee-stage bill that would levy service fees on “authorised” ships. Tehran says explicitly it does not reopen the strait. With ~7.8 mb of contracted barrels and 20–24 weeks of cushion, the reserve reaches its floor well before a 30–60 day temporary lane becomes a permanent settlement.

→ Implication · price a permitted, revocable, fee-bearing lane as what it is. A futures rally on this text is not the deficit closing.

Three things in the DOE ledger. It reconciles — net monthly movements imply an end-2025 stock of 413.4 mb against the 413.46 mb the Bloomberg series prints. The two streams share an originator, so this is an internal-consistency check, but the cumulative-drawn figure now rests on a flow ledger rather than a difference of stocks. The return leg is empty — zero exchange-return barrels in every month from March onward, on a loan with a repayment premium, and the GAO's finding that the reserve accepts returning crude at only 56% of specification applies to a repayment leg that is entirely prospective. And one number is unresolved — DOE's legend defines a Release No. 3 that no month carries. If it sits outside the ~133.5 mb contracted, the ~7.8 mb residual understates what DOE could deliver.

What this implies

The price has detached from DOE entirely. The August path — −8% on a called-off strike, +11.5% on a conditioned reopening, then four straight sessions higher into the memorandum’s expiry — owes nothing to a reserve that drew 5.3 mb over the same stretch. Headline exposure should be sized as though the buffer were absent, because as a price-setting instrument it is. Barrels are moving; Iranian barrels are not. Aramco resumed VLCC loadings inside the strait and Murban holds a ~$7 premium to Brent, while China’s imports of Iranian crude nearly halved to 534 kb/d. The structural response is now funded: TotalEnergies backed an expansion of the Habshan–Fujairah bypass to 1.8 mb/d, roughly doubling bypass capacity within a year.

03Outlook — whose clock binds

Two clocks have stopped, one expired on 17 August, and the only one still running is physical.

The US buffer clock is now a backstop, not a countdown. ~7.8 mb of contracted deliveries remain of the ~133.5 mb awarded, with no replacement since the June tender for 40 mb drew bids on 0.5, and no tender, award or refill announcement in the week to 27 August. The ~200 mb operational floor sits 89.7 mb below current stock — 20–24 weeks depending on the pace window. Total US crude, commercial plus SPR, stands at 718.6 mb, with commercial crude flat at 428.9 mb in the week to 21 August — 1% above its five-year average even as the strategic reserve makes a new low. August has shown this clock no longer sets the price: crude rose 11.5% when the Oman route arrived conditioned, ran to $92.67 into the memorandum's expiry, then gave back 6.2% in three sessions on a sanctions package that spared oil and a corridor Tehran says changes nothing — none of it on DOE, which drew 9 mb over the same fortnight without moving the tape.

Which floor? Three numbers circulate. The statutory EPCA reference of 252.4 mb binds only the Secretary's limited-drawdown authority, not the exchange authority used here — now ~37 mb above current stock. DOE puts the cavern-mechanics floor at ~70 mb. This paper uses the ~200 mb Tecity operational estimate, on the view that a reserve able only to trickle barrels out of half-empty caverns is not a usable buffer — a view the GAO supports, with drawdown capability at 61% of design before ~126 mb came out. The dispute is now public: Hochstein said on 15 August that going below 300 damages the caverns; DOE rebutted that “the caverns are always full — all that changes is the ratio of oil and water.” The reserve is already below 290.

The global buffer is worse than the American one. The IEA's August report puts observed global inventories down 69 mb in July to below 7.9 bn barrels and 410 mb since the war began, ~2.7 mb/day. The US SPR's 122 mb is roughly 30% of that — one national reserve accounting for nearly a third of world destocking, which is this paper's thesis restated in someone else's data. The agency sees a 1.8 mb/day Q3 deficit, closed by cutting demand rather than finding barrels.

The diplomatic clock restarted, narrowly. The 60-day memorandum lapsed on 17 August with the talks never having begun. Washington pivoted to economic coercion on 24 August with “Operation Economic Outcast” — ~60 designations across shipping, brokers and the shadow fleet — but it removed no barrels and named no major Chinese bank, and the market read it as lighter than feared. Then on 25–26 August Iran announced a temporary seven-mile corridor agreed with Oman: inbound entirely through Iranian territorial waters, 30–60 days pending a permanent arrangement, with an IRGC spokesman saying shares of the waters and the revenues are settled. Oman has announced nothing; coordinates, start date and revenue mechanics are undisclosed; a committee-stage bill would levy service fees on “authorised” ships; and Gharibabadi said on 25 August the strait does not reopen until Washington honours the June memorandum. The window the paper has tracked since v2 closed. What arrived in its place is a toll booth.

The Iranian clock — rerouting, but not replacing. Kharg Island reactivated on 12 August after a 25-day halt and Caspian shipments from Russia have roughly tripled since the blockade began, but the volume has not followed: China’s imports of Iranian crude fell to 534 kb/d in August from 823 in July against a 1.4 mb/d 2025 average. The rial hit a record 2.02 m to the dollar on 24 August. Iran is rerouting around the blockade; the reroute is not replacing the barrels — which is the context for blacklisting 45–46 Gulf tankers and threatening confiscation.

Products run on a separate clock underneath. Gasoline is $4.83 (25 Aug), still grinding higher while crude round-tripped, and Trump has begun asking Americans to accept higher pump prices. Energy-led inflation peaked at 4.2% y/y in May and eased to 3.4% in July — a lag that means no Hormuz outcome reaches the pump on the timeline the politics assumes. Product stocks are the sharper stress: gasoline drew 2.5 mb in the week to 21 August to ~14% below the five-year average, even as distillate built 2.2 mb. If a dislocation arrives, it is likelier to present as a diesel crisis than a crude one.

What this implies

What remains live is the physical reopening clock — mines, escorts, insurers, ~600 stranded ships. That is the variable to watch, and transit counts read it more honestly than headlines do.

04Risk — what would confirm or falsify

The falsification test set in v2.10 fired on both legs inside one week. This is what replaces it.

The suppression thesis has substantially played out. The draw ran to a 43-year low and through it, the interim deal collapsed, and with ~7.8 mb of contracted barrels left the reserve is effectively spent. The central prediction is realised in the order specified: the barrels ran out, the mask came off at the closed strait first and the Atlantic second, and tightness surfaced in structure as the buffer emptied rather than after. The open question is no longer whether the suppression ends, but whether the market is under-pricing a strait that stays physically shut while the long end prices comfort.

Soft branch recovering

  • A signed text with maritime control resolved and mines clearing on schedule
  • Fired in Brent basis narrowing with the outright falling — but not in Dubai, which widened

Hard branch winning

  • Present memorandum expired with talks never begun; the corridor is temporary and Tehran denies it reopens the strait
  • Partial basins now diverging — Dubai widened to −$4.82 while Brent flattened to −$0.90
  • Reversed outright fell 6.2% from its 21 Aug peak on sanctions relief and the corridor
  • Present SPR still drawing — −3.7 mb to 289.7, with ~7.8 mb contracted left

Exhaustion risk — still primary, and the test needs restating. Earlier versions treated compressing backwardation as the soft branch returning. That was wrong as written: compression has arrived with a lapsed memorandum, attacked ships and vessels turning back. The signal is not the spread narrowing but which leg narrows it. Compression from a falling front is the soft branch; compression from a rising back, as now, is the market extending the disruption in time. Restated last week: only a basis narrowing while the outright falls, alongside transits sustained in the dozens, returns the soft branch. Half of that fired this week — Brent’s basis narrowed as the outright fell 6.2% — but Dubai widened to −$4.82 and no transit count for the week was published. The test is passing in the basin furthest from the strait and failing in the one closest to it, which is the ordering the paper has argued throughout.

Positioning risk — beginning to correct. A curve turned at the front but anchored at the back is the classic pre-dislocation shape. The back has now begun to move: both bases compressed because the front outran spot, not because the prompt eased. That is the market pricing a failure it had treated as temporary. The correction is early — the long end still sits well below the prompt — but a market repricing the back while the strait stays shut is repricing the disruption’s duration, not its severity.

Optical-deal risk — realised. The corridor is the optical branch: a temporary, fee-bearing, permission-based lane that Tehran says is not a reopening, on which crude nonetheless fell 2.6%. The detection signal from here is unchanged — if the Dubai basis fails to normalise and transits stay thin, the deficit has not closed regardless of what the tape does. Dubai widened to −$4.82 the same week, which is the signal firing immediately.

Escalation risk. A tanker was disabled by a projectile off Oman on 24 August; Rezaei threatened to halt flows entirely if neighbouring states join the US economic campaign; and Gharibabadi warned that US mine-detection vessels would make “very good targets.” Discount definitive “strait open” claims from either side until transit counts confirm them.

What this implies

The asymmetry runs the other way from March. Then the reserve capped the upside and the risk was paying for a premium DOE could suppress. Now the front is bid, the long end is not, and there is no instrument left to flatten a dislocation. Whatever the directional view, the distribution has fattened on one side only.

A reserve sized by stock–flow thinking is now defended by exploiting the gap between stock and flow — but the same barrel cannot be both spent into the spot market and held as a buffer. The signal and the supply are spent in the same act. Conservation of matter sets the clock.

Conventions. Prices, spreads, SPR levels and gasoline come only from the Tecity Bloomberg pull; news and context are web-sourced and separately verified. Brent front-month = CO1; basis = front minus spot, so positive is contango and negative backwardation. Dubai = DBL1−DBLA. SPR = DOESSPR. Operational floor = the ~200 mb Tecity estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb conventionally cited; cushion = level minus 200.
