Abstract

In March 2026 the U.S. authorized a 172-million-barrel release from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, structured as an exchange (loan-with-premium) rather than a sale, as its share of a coordinated 400-million-barrel IEA action against the Strait of Hormuz disruption. This paper reconstructs, from the EIA weekly inventory series, market spreads, and intraday tape, the gap between the program's announced parameters (172 mb; ~1.43 mb/day) and its realized execution (~84 mb delivered through 19 Jun; ~0.9 mb/day overall, peaking near ~1.30–1.43 mb/day, easing, then running back to ~1.28 in the week to 12 Jun). The release is shown to be physically incapable of clearing a 9–13 mb/day net Hormuz shortfall on its own. Its observable function, refined in v2, is active suppression: simultaneously pressing the prompt physical market (SPR barrels into spot) and the forward curve (announcement effect), aided by suppressed Chinese imports — three downward pressures on a market that would otherwise reveal the deficit. The compression is dated: remaining contracted SPR deliveries exhaust in late July / first week August at recent draw rates, which is also when independent analysts forecast Chinese demand to resume. The diplomatic resolution that would prevent reconciliation through dislocation has not arrived; a 60-day framework (ceasefire extension, Hormuz reopening, mine-clearing) has been tabled, sent back in early June over the maritime-control point, and then collapsed into renewed U.S.–Iran strikes by 9–11 June, with Iran re-declaring the strait closed (disputed by U.S. Central Command) while the futures curve flipped into backwardation across every grade (Dubai deepest, ~−$5) even as front-month Brent eased to ~$91 at settlement — the tightness showing in curve structure before price level. Then, on 11 June, the pendulum swung back: the President cancelled planned strikes and announced a "great settlement," a memorandum he himself called "a little conceptual." It resolved quickly: an interim MOU was declared complete ~14–15 June and signed electronically ~17 June at the G7 sidelines (the 19 June Switzerland technical round was then postponed, with a "roadmap" toward a final deal reached 21–22 June) — reopening Hormuz, lifting the blockades, granting Iran ~$300bn and sanctions relief while deferring the nuclear file to a 60-day window. Brent collapsed ~14% on the week to ~$79 (−38% from the April peak), the whole curve repricing toward ~$80. The reopening is gradual — hundreds of ships stranded, mines to clear, 30 days to prewar traffic — so this is the optical branch the paper flagged, not a clean soft resolution; and the SPR draw held near its peak into the de-escalation — the first post-deal print (week ending 19 June, released 24 June) came in at −9.1 mb to 331.2 mb, the SPR's lowest since 1983, even with the deal signed; the draw then tapered (−5.5, −6.2, −3.0 mb) to 316.5 mb by 10 July — the lowest since April 1983. But in that same fortnight the interim deal collapsed: the US revoked Iran's oil authorisation, struck Iran (8 and 12–14 July) and reinstated its naval blockade, Iran re-closed Hormuz, and front-month Brent snapped back to ~$83 (intraday high ~$86, ~+19% above pre-war) as transits fell >50%. The war has since widened — nine consecutive nights of US strikes, US troops killed in Iran's attack on a base in Jordan, and a Houthi maritime embargo on Saudi Arabia opening a new front — and Brent spiked to a ~$94 peak (23 Jul, the highest since 2022) before a late-July strikes pause pulled it back to ~$87 as the SPR drained past 307.6 mb. In the first week of August the premium drained out altogether: Trump called off a planned mass strike (1 Aug), announced talks (3 Aug), and a 60-day Iran–Oman lane arrangement took shape — Brent fell ~8% to ~$79 before Iran's own restrictive draft transit plan (6 Aug) pushed it back to $82.49, and the Brent basis flipped out of backwardation into +$2.03 contango, the first since early June. The SPR meanwhile drew to 304.8 mb (31 Jul), ~22 mb of contracted barrels left. With the buffer near its floor, the suppression can no longer hold the price down in either direction: what now moves crude is the diplomacy, and the paper's core claim — that the signal and the supply are spent in the same act — stands. The reconciliation the paper mapped is arriving not as a spike but as its mirror image: a market that has stopped paying for prompt barrels while the strait is still shut and the reserve is nearly empty. The crude reconciliation, the SPR contracted exhaustion, and the contested ceasefire window still converge on the same six-to-eight-week period — a configuration the paper terms the contested deadline — but the dislocation has begun arriving early, via diplomatic breakdown rather than buffer exhaustion.

01Summary

The argument in one screen — v2.10

A strategic reserve is a stock; a supply crisis is a problem of flow. The 2026 SPR release cannot resolve the deficit at any plausible delivery rate; what it can do is buy time by pressing both ends of the futures curve down at once. v2 evidences this directly.

(i) The headline is a ceiling, not a delivery. Of 172 mb authorized, ~133 mb contracted across four tenders, ~84 mb physically delivered through 19 Jun. Realized lift averaged ~0.9 mb/day overall; the late-May weeks reached the advertised ~1.30–1.43 mb/day pace, eased, then the week to 12 Jun ran back up to ~1.28.

(ii) The drawdown ran at peak rate, briefly eased, then re-accelerated. Weekly draws of −8.6, −9.9, −9.1, −8.0, −7.9, then −8.9 mb (12 Jun), −9.1 mb (19 Jun), −5.5 mb (26 Jun), −6.2 mb (3 Jul) −3.0 mb (10 Jul) −5.1 mb (17 Jul), −3.8 mb (24 Jul) and −2.8 mb (31 Jul) to 304.8 mb — a deepening 43-year low, ~111 mb drawn since the war began. The dip to ~8 mb (late May/early June) looked like a benign taper; the week to 12 Jun put that to rest, re-accelerating as the war premium collapsed and the deal came together. DOE drawing faster as the crisis resolves is the opposite of a supply remedy winding down — it reads as pressing the price down or rushing to finish deliveries. The draw continued through the late-July pause and into the August talks; with the buffer near its floor (~22 mb contracted left) the suppression is no longer what sets the price — Brent round-tripped ~$94 → ~$82 → ~$87, then fell to ~$79 (5 Aug) and settled $82.49 (6 Aug) on the diplomacy alone.

(iii) Deep contango, then a June backwardation flip across every grade. On the Bloomberg futures basis (front-month minus spot), the grades nearest Hormuz showed the prompt crushed below the future through late May — Dubai front-month $11–16 above spot, outright contango — the mask of a deficit held down by SPR supply and absent Chinese demand. From 4 June the curve flips the other way, into backwardation across all four grades: Dubai deepest (front ~$5 below spot, 4–10 Jun), Brent and Oman milder (~−$1.5 to −$2.5) and easing into 11 June. A prompt premium is the tightness signature the hard branch predicts — now measured in the corrected feed, not inferred. Through 11 June the dislocation showed in curve structure ahead of price level. Then the deal resolved it the other way: with the agreement signed, the level collapsed — front-month Brent ~−14% on the week to ~$79, the whole curve to ~$80 (Figure 4) — the clean Brent front-month-minus-spot flipped into backwardation peaking at −$6.4 (23 Jul), eased to −$2.15 (30 Jul) as strikes paused, and then flipped back into contango at +$2.03 (6 Aug) — the first contango since early June, and the cleanest single sign that the market has stopped pricing prompt scarcity. Dubai's contango widened over the same week from +$3.03 to +$4.64. The outright level round-tripped from ~$71 (2 Jul) to a ~$94 peak (23 Jul, highest since 2022), to ~$82 (28 Jul), ~$87 (30 Jul), ~$79 (5 Aug) and $82.49 (6 Aug) / ~$83.6 (7 Aug). Note the asymmetry the paper has flagged throughout: the strait is still shut and the reserve is nearly empty, yet the curve now prices comfort. That is a positioning fact, not a physical one.

(iv) The contested deadline resolved — by a deal, not by exhaustion. The 60-day MOU (19 Jun) collapsed into war, now in a fragile pause. After the 31 Jul print ~22 mb of contracted SPR remains (~111 mb lifted) — roughly six weeks at the recent ~3–4 mb/week pace — a near-empty backstop even as the talks resume: neither the pause nor the August diplomacy has refilled the reserve or reopened the strait. So the diplomatic clock stopped first. What remains live is the physical reopening clock — 30 days to prewar Hormuz traffic, mines to clear, ~600 ships to move — and the buffer clock as a backstop if that reopening stalls.

(v) Retail products are a separate, structural clock — now feeding the macro. US average gasoline (BBG) eased only marginally to ~$4.74 by 5 Aug from ~$4.76 at end-July (still below the $5.47 all-time high) — the crude round-trip has not reached the pump, atop the structural refining picture (seven closures since 2019, ~92% utilization, lowest finished-fuel inventories since 2000). Even so, energy-led U.S. inflation had pushed above 4% during the war — and the structural refining deficit means a clean crude resolution still does not deliver full retail relief; products remain the slow, separate clock.

The SPR exchange is not failing to be a supply remedy by accident — it was never structured to be one. Its function is active price suppression: pressing the prompt market down with finite physical barrels and the forward curve down with announcement effects, in service of buying time for diplomacy. The barrels and the signal are spent in the same act. When the barrels run out, the signal dies with them.

The contested deadline has now resolved — by a signed deal (19 Jun), ahead of the buffer. But the resolution is optical in shape: a 14-point MOU that reopens Hormuz on paper, hands Iran ~$300bn and sanctions relief, and defers the nuclear file 60 days. The clocks did not all stop. The U.S. supply-buffer clock keeps ticking (SPR drawn to 304.8 mb — a deepening 43-year low — ~22 mb of contracted barrels left, still drawing ~3 mb/week through the pause and the talks) and the Iranian revenue/blockade clock (export losses every day Hormuz stays shut) — which the deal is meant to stop. The market that spent late May pricing the soft outcome got it on 14–19 June and ran with it: Brent −14% on the week, the whole curve to ~$80. The scoreboard turned toward resolution. The catch the paper has flagged from the start is that a signed, conceptual MOU is not the same as oil moving freely through Hormuz — and the deal has now collapsed with the buffer nearly spent: the US struck Iran again (8 & 12–14 Jul), reinstated its naval blockade and Iran re-closed the strait, so the suppression can no longer cap the price — Brent round-tripped to a ~$94 peak, dipped to ~$82 on the pause, and sits ~$83 as a second, narrower arrangement is negotiated. The August draft is the same catch in sharper form: a lane scheme that reopens Hormuz on paper under Iranian and Omani management, with Iran's own text barring US and Israeli ships and fining violators 20% of cargo value, is not oil moving freely.

02Thought Process

How the question unfolded — extended in v2

Stage 1 — The accounting question

Was the exchanged oil still booked as SPR inventory while physically gone? Directionally the instinct was right (an exchange retains a government claim recorded as a receivable), but the suspicion landed in the wrong ledger. The EIA series is physical and draws down on lifting.

Stage 2 — The execution forensics

Reading the four tenders against the weekly inventory series revealed the awarded-versus-delivered gap and the realized lift rate — roughly half the advertised pace for most of the program, only reaching the plan in its latest weeks. The advertised "1.43 mb/day" was real as a plan; delivery only approached it as the program matured.

Stage 3 — The scale test

Setting realized lift against the net Hormuz shortfall (~9–13 mb/day) showed the release covers a fraction of the gap for a limited duration. A physical remedy it is not.

Stage 4 — The mechanism & the clock

If not supply, then signal: the program suppresses the forward curve and buys time. That reframes "when does it end?" as "when are the physical and expectations markets forced to reconcile?" — a question answered by the prompt-versus-deferred spread.

Stage 5 (new in v2) — The suppression mechanism, visible

The late-May spread data and the SPR prints together force the v2 conclusion. The Gulf-grade curve did not behave as v1's "convergence" framing predicted — through late May the prompt was pressed below the future (Dubai front-month $11–16 over spot), outright contango, while DOE drew at the design ceiling: both spot and futures pressed down at once — SPR barrels into the prompt market, forward-curve guidance for the paper market, suppressed Chinese demand removing global pull. The contested deadline has now settled that question the other way. The early-June backwardation flip was not a managed hand-off to a real deal; within days the framework was sent back, the talks collapsed, and the war resumed — and the futures curve flipped into backwardation across every grade, the prompt premium the suppression had masked finally surfacing (Dubai ~−$5). Tellingly, outright Brent only eased to ~$91 over the same days: the dislocation is emerging in curve structure first, while the still-substantial buffer caps the level. The calm was bought; when the diplomacy the barrels were buying time for failed to arrive, the suppression began to give way to the tightness it had deferred — surfacing in the structure, via diplomatic breakdown ahead of, not because of, the buffer running dry. The resolution then arrived the way the paper had marked most likely and most under-priced: not a clean soft settlement but an interim, optical one — a signed MOU that reopens Hormuz on paper, pays Iran, and defers the nuclear file — on which the market nonetheless priced a full resolution, collapsing crude ~14% on the week to ~$79. And in the final irony for the supply-remedy story, DOE did not ease into the de-escalation; it drew faster (−8.9 mb) as the war premium came out — which the post-deal print (19 Jun) has now answered: it held at peak, −9.1 mb to 331.2 mb.

Stage 6 (new in v2) — The contested deadline

If the suppression is active and finite, then its duration is the strategic variable. Both negotiating parties can compute the buffer-exhaustion date from public data. Iran's incentive is to delay any concession until that date approaches and U.S. leverage decays; the U.S. incentive (per Trump's "we'll out-wait him") is to deny that any concession is forthcoming on Iran's clock. The leaked-but-mutually-disowned MOU was sized to 60 days — almost exactly the remaining contracted SPR delivery window. The market structure and the diplomatic structure are now visibly synchronized to the same date.

The prompt-physical spread — compressed past convergence

The deep Gulf-grade contango is the cleanest single piece of evidence in the paper. A genuine 9–13 mb/day net Hormuz deficit cannot coexist with the grades nearest Hormuz trading $11–16 below their own front-month. The only way both are true at once is if the prompt is being masked by active physical supply (SPR barrels reaching the spot window) and absent demand (China) — both dated, both finite. On the Bloomberg basis the effect is larger than the prior Dated read showed. The early-June unwind toward backwardation, occurring as the whole complex falls rather than rises, is consistent with the suppression being relaxed into a market pricing de-escalation — not with a deficit reasserting. That distinction is the soft-vs-hard fork, and it turns on whether a deal actually signs.

The war-premium round-trip

The clearest picture of the whole episode is the outright front-month itself. Brent began the year in the low-$60s on an oversupplied, pre-war market; the 28 February war and the Hormuz closure drove it to a ~$112 front-month peak (Dated Brent spiked far higher); the SPR release, suppressed Chinese demand, and recurring deal-hope then ground it back down through the spring; the 14–19 June agreement collapsed the remaining premium, returning Brent to ~$79 by mid-June; but the deal itself then broke down in early July — US strikes, the naval blockade reinstated, Hormuz re-closed — and the premium snapped back and then surged — Brent to ~$83 (16 July) and a ~$94 peak (23 July, the highest since 2022) as the war widened — before a late-July strikes pause knocked it to ~$82 (28 July); it settled ~$87 (30 July). Then, in the first week of August, the premium drained out: Trump called off a planned mass strike (1 Aug) and announced talks (3 Aug), Treasury Secretary Bessent floated a Hormuz deal “as soon as Wednesday,” and Brent fell ~8% to ~$79 (5 Aug) — before Iran's own restrictive draft transit plan pushed it back up 3.8% to $82.49 (6 Aug) and ~$83.6 (7 Aug).

On 28 May, WTI front-month and Brent front-month rallied in lockstep on truce headlines to +4%, then faded together as the framework was disowned. The two benchmarks moved as one across the session — the signature of a market pricing a single global signal (the diplomatic clock) rather than independent fundamentals. The fade pattern repeats the cycle observed since March: rally on resolution-hope, fade as substance fails to arrive. Each cycle consumes barrels and credibility.

05Outlook — Two clocks, one window

The contested deadline has passed — what binds now is the reopening regime

The v1 framing — soft landing vs. hard reconciliation — is preserved but reorganized. The decisive variable is no longer "when do buffers exhaust" in the abstract; it is whose clock binds first in the contested deadline.

The U.S. supply-buffer clock

Remaining contracted SPR deliveries: ~22 mb of the ~133 mb awarded after the −2.8 mb week to 31 Jul. At the recent ~3–4 mb/week pace that would exhaust in ~6 weeks — but the buffer stopped being the binding clock some time ago; it is now a backstop, and a thin one. Behind it, the operational floor — revised to ~200 mb on the Tecity in-house estimate (analyst Zilin), above the ~150 mb level conventionally cited — sits only ~105 mb below the current 304.8 mb stock (a deepening 43-year low); commercial crude continues to draw, with total US crude stocks at 730.8 mb, the lowest since 1984, so there is materially less cushion behind the SPR than the standard floor implies. Independent forecast (DeLaura, Rabobank): Chinese demand resumes mid-July, removing the third suppressor. What v2.3 changes is the trigger: the dislocation no longer waits on buffer exhaustion. The 9–11 June swings — collapse and re-closure, then a sudden deal announcement — show the reconciliation can be forced (or deferred) by headlines well before the buffer empties, with stock still ~25 mb from contracted-empty. The buffer clock now sets the ceiling on how long any renewed suppression could even be attempted, not the timing of the break, which diplomacy is driving day to day.

The first week of August is the cleanest demonstration yet of that last point. With ~22 mb of contracted barrels left — too little to move a global market — crude fell ~8% in three sessions on a called-off strike and an announced negotiation, then rose 3.8% in one session on a parliamentary draft. Neither move had anything to do with DOE. The buffer no longer sets the price; it only sets how badly the United States would be exposed if the negotiation fails, and that exposure is now roughly six weeks of contracted deliveries and ~105 mb of cushion above the Tecity floor.

The Iranian revenue/blockade clock

Iran is bleeding export revenue every day Hormuz stays shut and the U.S. naval blockade holds. Iran has requested unfrozen access to $12bn in frozen assets in the MOU — i.e. liquidity to span its clock. Aramco's ~100 mb/week global supply loss is matched by Iran's own stranded exports. Iran's clock binds whenever its FX/revenue position becomes politically unsustainable, which is unobservable from outside but is on its own decay path.

The three endgames

Soft (diplomatic, full) — partially realized, execution pending: the 60-day MOU signs and delivers genuine free passage (no tolls, mines cleared, insurers treating the strait as open), crude reconciles to the deferred curve's ~$65–70 anchor, products stay structurally elevated. This was the late-May base case the ~20% crude drop priced; it receded into the 9–10 June strikes, then returned on 11 June when the President announced a "great settlement" and oil fell ~3%. But the full soft case requires the signed text to deliver genuine free passage — and the announced MOU is "conceptual," unconfirmed by Iran, with Israel not a party. So the tape is again pricing a deal, but whether it is the soft deal (real reopening) or merely the optical one is exactly the unresolved fork.

Middle (optical) — this is what landed: the MOU signs and headline-reopens Hormuz, but the reopening is partial — escorted, toll-laden, slow-to-clear-mines, or kept under Iranian "management" — so physical free flow lags the announcement. Futures rally on the signing; the suppression props (SPR, China) lift on schedule into a market whose physical deficit hasn't actually closed. The market briefly believes the deal is the resolution before discovering it is not. This is the configuration the curve is least equipped to recognize, and v2.2 raises its weight: the live sticking point is precisely maritime control, and an experienced commodities voice (ICMA's Parker) has publicly warned that even if Hormuz opens, the opening will likely be only partial, citing refinery damage, depleted inventories, and tanker-security frictions. The reported terms (no tolls, 30-day mine-clearing) describe the soft case; whether the signed text and the physical execution deliver it is the fork. This is what landed. The signed MOU (19 Jun) reopens Hormuz on paper and the market priced a clean resolution — Brent to ~$79, the curve to ~$80 — but the physical substance (escort terms, mine-clearing, insurer confidence, ~600 stranded ships, a 30-day timeline) is only beginning. The paper had flagged this as the under-priced case; the gap between a signed conceptual MOU and oil actually moving freely is exactly where the residual risk now sits. The August negotiation has made that gap explicit rather than closing it. The reported 60-day Iran–Oman arrangement is a managed-lane regime by construction — inbound through Iranian waters, outbound through Omani waters, a joint coordination centre, the mined median lane deferred 30 days — and Iran's own published draft would bar US and Israeli ships and fine violators 20% of cargo value. That is the optical branch written into the text of the deal itself, not merely a risk that the execution disappoints. An outside forecast (Stavridis, 4 Aug) independently put this “dual-key” structure at the modal outcome of the ~60% negotiation path. If it signs, the correct reading of a futures rally is not that the deficit closed but that the market has agreed to price a permitted, partial, politically revocable flow as though it were free passage.

Hard (physical) — flared 9–10 Jun, now dormant; the live tail-risk is a reopening that stalls: the 9–10 June collapse pushed hard toward this branch before the buffer ran dry — the curve detached at the prompt (backwardation across all grades, Dubai deepest ~−$5) — then the deal collapsed the level instead. The branch is not gone but inverted in form: with the market now priced for clean resolution, the hard outcome would arrive as a snap-back if Hormuz fails to physically reopen — one analyst on the record warning of a move into the mid-to-high $100s later this summer if normal transit does not materialize. Front-month Brent eased to ~$91 (the intraday $95 spike faded); refined-product and macro pressure show in energy-led inflation above 4%. The tightness is in structure ahead of level. The distinctive v2.3 point: this branch is being triggered by diplomatic breakdown and renewed closure, not by SPR exhaustion — the buffer still has ~22 mb of contracted barrels. So the early dislocation lands into a market still partly cushioned, but one whose cushion is finite and whose positioning was set for resolution — the source of the violence if it continues.

The product clock — running independently underneath

Whichever crude branch resolves, U.S. refined products are on their own structural clock: EIA-forecast 2026 inventories at 2000-era lows, ~92% utilization, no spare capacity, seven major closures since 2019. A clean Hormuz deal cannot deliver consumer relief on the timeline a soft-landing crude resolution would imply. This decouples the political payoff of a deal from the diplomatic incentive to sign one — and arguably reduces the threshold at which the U.S. accepts unfavorable terms, since the deal does not buy as much retail relief as in past precedent.

06Risk Assessment

What would confirm or falsify the suppression and contested-deadline reads

The suppression thesis has now fully played out. The draw tapered toward a 43-year low, the interim deal collapsed, and the war widened — nine nights of US strikes, US troops killed, a Houthi embargo on Saudi Arabia — while Brent spiked to a ~$94 peak (23 Jul, highest since 2022) with the backwardation at −$6.4 before a late-July strikes pause eased both (Brent ~$87, backwardation −$2.15). With ~22 mb of contracted barrels left the reserve is effectively spent, and the first week of August showed what that means in practice: the price is now set entirely by diplomacy, moving ~8% down on a called-off strike and announced talks and ~4% back up on a restrictive Iranian draft, with the Brent basis flipping into +$2.03 contango — the prompt premium fully drained. The paper's central prediction is realized in both directions: once the barrels ran low, the price detached from DOE and attached to the negotiating table. The open question is no longer whether the suppression ends but whether the market is now under-pricing a strait that remains physically shut while the curve prices comfort.

Tells the soft branch is recovering. A signed MOU (not a "fully negotiated" claim amid strikes) with the maritime-control point resolved and mines clearing on schedule; a sustained ceasefire holding through more than a single news cycle; the outright price falling back toward the high-$80s; gasoline resuming its decline once the escalation washes through.

Tells the hard branch is winning (several now present). Talks collapsed and the strait re-declared closed (present); the Gulf-grade curve already in backwardation, Dubai ~−$5 (present — the leading structural tell); next would be that backwardation deepening and broadening with outright Brent turning up (so far the level has eased to ~$91, so this leg is not yet present); SPR draws snapping back toward 9 mb/week as the floor nears (not yet — draw still ~8); Cushing toward minimums; product cracks blowing out; energy-led inflation climbing further from the >4% already printed.

Optical-deal risk (the under-priced middle case). A signed MOU that headline-reopens Hormuz without delivering physical free passage would trigger a futures rally while leaving the deficit broadly intact. Detection signal: a deal is announced but the post-roll Dubai/Oman spread does not normalize and tanker transits stay thin — the Asian physical market shows the truth first, because partial/escorted/tolled reopening appears in transit counts before headlines. Watch the Hormuz transit tally and the Dubai/Oman spread as the two independent verifiers of any announcement. ICMA's Parker has already flagged a partial opening as the likely case.

Complacency risk (new, and now the primary one). The Brent basis has flipped from a −$6.4 prompt premium to +$2.03 contango in a fortnight while the strait is still shut, the Houthi campaign against Saudi shipping has widened to the northern Red Sea, and the SPR has ~22 mb of contracted barrels left. A curve that pays to store, in that physical configuration, is pricing the deal as done. The falsification test is specific and cheap to watch: if the lane arrangement signs and Hormuz transits do not climb out of single digits within two to three weeks, or if Iran's parliamentary draft (ship bans, 20%-of-cargo penalties) survives into the operative text, then the contango is mispriced and the prompt premium should return violently — into a market that has just removed its hedges. Conversely, a genuine climb in transits toward the pre-war ~100/day would validate the contango and complete the soft branch.

Positioning risk. A forward curve still anchored to resolution — the long end pinned near ~$65–70 — leaves the market structurally under-hedged for failure. This is starting to show in the curve, not yet the level: the front has flipped to backwardation (prompt premium, Dubai ~−$5) while the long end holds its ~$65–70 anchor and outright Brent has only eased to ~$91 — the structural precursor to the violent front-end move, with the move itself still ahead if the prompt premium broadens.

Head-fake risk (now bilateral). The rally-fade-deny cycle repeated since March and reached its sharpest form on 11 Jun — but this time it terminated in an actual signing (19 Jun), so the head-fake risk has largely discharged. The residual is the mirror image: a signed but conceptual/interim MOU is now being treated as a done deal, when the physical reopening (mines, escorts, insurers, ~600 stranded ships) is what determines whether the priced resolution holds. Discount both "deal imminent" and definitive "strait closed/open" claims (Iran asserted closure; US CENTCOM denied) until confirmed by both sides and by tanker-transit data, not headlines.

Escalation risk. Combat continued through the talks (US strikes in southern Iran; Iranian ballistic missiles toward Kuwait), raising the probability that the U.S. abandons negotiation for force if the clock runs out — which removes Iran's leverage but does not necessarily reopen Hormuz quickly.

Partial-reopening risk. An intermittent, escorted, tolled, or "Iranian-managed" reopening smears the binary into a prolonged muddy tightening, stretching the timeline and blunting both the supply-buffer urgency and the diplomatic incentive to settle. This is the same configuration as the optical branch and is now the live sticking point.

Data-latency, revision and data-integrity risk.
